1.2

Is there…? and Are there…?

What we will learn: Distinguish asking whether a place exists from asking where an identified place is.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

One place

Is there + a/an + singular noun?
There is a museum. There isn’t a bank on the example map.

Plural places

Are there + any + plural noun?
There are some places to eat. There aren’t any banks on the map.

Existence and location

Is there a museum?
Asks whether one exists in the area.

Where is the museum?
Asks for its location once the place is identified.

Use is with a singular place and are with plural places. The statement about banks refers to this map, not to every town or a real city.

02

Giving and Understanding Directions

Polite questions, movement, orientation and a complete worked route.

2.1

Asking for directions politely

What we will learn: Recognise useful questions for location, a route and clarification.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Ask for a location

Excuse me politely opens the exchange. The main question asks for a location.

Ask for a route

How + can + subject + base verb?
From here identifies the starting point in the conversation.

Clarify information

The first asks to hear the information again. The second checks a particular step.

A useful directions exchange makes the destination and starting point clear. The examples are complete language models, rather than role-play instructions.

2.2

Movement and direction words

What we will learn: Understand the actions expressed by short direction instructions.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Go straight and turn

Go straight ahead: continue in the direction you face.
Turn left / turn right: change direction.
At Hill Street: the place where the turn happens.

Along and past

Along: follow the street.
Past: continue beyond a place or junction.

Across and opposite

Across here describes movement from one side to the other.

The museum is across from the station.
Across from describes a location.

These instructions usually begin with a base verb: go, walk, turn. The subject you is understood. They illustrate the language used to explain a route.

2.3

Starting point and viewpoint

What we will learn: Distinguish the fixed orientation of a map from a traveller’s changing left and right.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A stated starting position

This is the starting position of the worked route. The traveller faces towards the top of the map.

The traveller’s left and right

The places lie ahead along the route, on those sides of Oak Street.

Facing south, those left/right relationships reverse.

A turn changes the viewpoint

West points towards the left edge of this map. The museum is north of Hill Street, which is the traveller’s right when facing west.

To the right of the museum on the map uses the map viewer’s perspective. On your right in a route uses the traveller’s current facing direction.

03

Tourist Information and Recommendations

Visitor questions, recommendations, guided tours and an explained itinerary.

3.1

Useful visitor information questions

What we will learn: Distinguish opening times, duration, distance and price in fully answered examples.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Opening times

“It opens at nine in the morning.”
What time does it close?
“It closes at five in the afternoon.”

Duration

“It lasts ninety minutes.” This asks about time, not distance.

Distance

“About five hundred metres by the walking route.” This is fictional example information; the diagram is not to scale.

Price

“It costs five dollars.” Does + cost in the question; costs in the answer.

All hours, distances, durations and prices are invented language examples. They describe the fictional information supplied here, not live services or travel offers.

3.2

Recommendations and options

What we will learn: Understand recommend + noun or -ing and distinguish an option from a recommendation.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Recommend + a place or thing

A noun phrase names the recommendation. Can introduces what the speaker is able to suggest.

Recommend + an activity

After recommend, visiting and walking name the activities.

Use I recommend visiting or You can visit, rather than “I recommend you to visit”.

You can… and You should…

Can: an available option.
Should: a recommendation about the visit.
Both are followed by a base verb.

A reason makes a recommendation clearer: You can visit the market because there are places to eat there. These examples apply language from earlier units to visitor information.

3.3

Understanding a guided tour

What we will learn: Recognise the meeting point, duration, stops and closing in a tour description.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Fictional walking tour

Meeting point: Tourist Office.
Departure: 10:00 a.m.
Duration: 90 minutes.
Price: five dollars.

Information a visitor needs

Where the group meets, what the tour includes, how long it lasts and where it ends.

A clear opening

Welcome to our town. identifies the guide’s role.
We meet at… gives a place and time.

A clear sequence

First, then, finally organise the stops.
The walk ends at the market identifies the last location.

A tour description can use the present simple to explain its usual sequence.

The guide model provides a complete description of an invented walking tour. It does not require a booking or ask the learner to lead a tour.

3.4

How an itinerary connects places and times

What we will learn: Recognise day, time, place and purpose in an explained weekend itinerary.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Fictional opening information

Museum: Tuesday–Sunday, 9 a.m.–5 p.m.; closed Monday.
Market: daily, 8 a.m.–6 p.m.
Park: daily, 6 a.m.–8 p.m.
Café: daily, 8 a.m.–7 p.m.

Complete example itinerary

Saturday, 9:30 a.m.: meet at the Tourist Office.
Saturday, 10 a.m.: visit the Museum.
Saturday, 12 noon: have lunch at the Market.
Sunday, 10 a.m.: walk in the Park.
Sunday, 12 noon: have lunch at the Café.

A plan and an arrangement

Going to presents an intention.
We’re meeting… presents the meeting as arranged in this example, connecting with Unit 4.

How the times fit

The Museum visit is on Saturday during its stated hours. Both lunches take place during the stated opening times. The Sunday walk is during the Park’s stated hours.

The itinerary combines places and times rather than listing destinations without context.

The timetable is complete and all details are fictional. The map supplies location relationships, while the information panel supplies opening times; the map alone does not supply hours or exact travel times.

4.2

Complete brochure and email models

What we will learn: Recognise how visitor information and a personal itinerary use the unit language.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

These are complete worked models for the fictional town. No information needs to be filled in or submitted.

Visitor brochure: complete model

How the brochure works

Location: opposite / between / across from.
Opening times: days and hours.
Options: You can visit / have lunch.
Further information: ask at the tourist office.

Weekend email: complete model

Hi Laura,

We’re going to spend the weekend in town. We’re meeting at the tourist office on Saturday at nine thirty. Then, we’re going to visit the museum. It opens at nine, so we can visit in the morning. At noon, we’re going to have lunch at the market. On Sunday morning, we can walk in the park and have lunch at the café afterwards. I can recommend bringing a camera if you enjoy taking photos.

Best wishes,
Mateo

How the email works

Greeting: Hi Laura.
Intention: We’re going to spend…
Arrangement: We’re meeting…
Sequence: then / at noon / on Sunday.
Reason: It opens at nine, so…
Recommendation: I can recommend bringing…
Closing: Best wishes.

4.3

Useful town and travel expressions

What we will learn: Distinguish phrasal verbs and an idiom and understand their meaning and context.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Phrasal verb

desplazarse por una zona

Travel from place to place within an area.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Get around town by bus or on foot.

Phrasal verb

mirar o explorar un lugar

Explore a place and see what is there.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Look around the market or look around on your own.

Phrasal verb

subir a un autobús, tren u otro transporte similar

Board a bus, train or similar vehicle.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Get on the bus; for a car, get in the car is the usual expression.

Phrasal verb

bajar de un autobús, tren u otro transporte similar

Leave a bus, train or similar vehicle.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Get off the bus; for a car, get out of the car is the usual expression.

Idiom

a muy poca distancia

A very short distance.

Use: Conversational, figurative. It gives an approximate sense of closeness, not a precise measurement.

The bus examples illustrate transport expressions and do not add a bus service or stop to the map. On foot means walking. Take the bus and travel by bus describe a transport option; get on/off describe boarding and leaving it.

4.4

Unit summary

What we will learn: Recognise the language connected by the location, route and visitor-information models.

  • I can understand names of places and shops around town.
  • I can distinguish asking whether a place exists from asking where it is.
  • I can describe location relationships using a supplied map.
  • I can distinguish a static location from a movement instruction.
  • I can understand a route from a stated starting point and facing direction.
  • I can ask for opening times, duration, distance and price.
  • I can understand recommendations with recommend + noun or -ing.
  • I can recognise the main information in a guided tour.
  • I can understand a complete brochure and a coherent weekend itinerary.
