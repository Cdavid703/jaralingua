01

Celebrations and Traditions

Key concepts, vocabulary, time and place, and traditions in context.

1.1

Celebration, festival, holiday and tradition

What we will learn: Distinguish four related words by their meaning and context.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

An occasion or event that marks something special, such as a birthday or an anniversary.

An organised event or series of events connected with a theme, community, culture or art.

A recognised special day, or a period away from work or study. The meaning depends on context.

A custom or practice passed on within a family or community.

An event and a repeated custom

The celebration is the occasion. Cooking together is the tradition in this fictional family.

Holiday and vacation

A public holiday is an officially recognised day. Its name alone does not tell us whether every business closes.

In British English, on holiday often means on vacation. In American English, vacation commonly expresses that meaning.

Words can overlap

A festival can include several celebrations and traditions. It may take place during a public holiday, but the words do not mean the same thing.

The examples describe a particular occasion or community. A tradition does not have to be shared by everyone in a country.

Useful combinations

Put up decorations · wear a costume · watch a parade · give a gift · attend a wedding.

Costume and clothes

Clothes is a general word. A costume has a particular performance, character or cultural purpose.

“People wear costumes” describes the event; it does not mean every person wears one.

1.3

Time, place and occasion

What we will learn: Recognise how in, on, at and for locate an event or connect a plan with an occasion.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

In + month or year

In December · in March · in 2027.
In answers a broad time question here.

On + day or named date

New Year’s Eve names the evening before New Year’s Day. On introduces the named day.

At + time or event

At seven · at midnight · at a festival · at a wedding.

For + occasion

For connects the plans with the occasion. Compare on New Year’s Eve, which identifies a day.

Natural questions include What do you usually do at a festival? and Where are you meeting your friends? Time and place phrases make the context clearer.

What a description can include

Reasons and categories

02

What People Usually Do

Repeated customs, quantities, sequences, similarities and differences.

2.1

Customs and frequency

What we will learn: Recognise the present simple and frequency expressions in descriptions of repeated customs.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A repeated custom

Present simple describes a repeated pattern. It does not locate one particular event in progress now.

Frequency position

Before the main verb: often invite.
After be: is usually.
This applies the frequency patterns from Unit 3.

Questions and negatives

“My family usually cooks at home.”

Does your family cook? → base verb.
My sister doesn’t wear a costume. → doesn’t + base verb.

Always describes every occasion within the situation stated. “We always have dinner together on this anniversary” is a family example, not a claim about everyone.

2.2

Describing what there is

What we will learn: Distinguish plural countable nouns from uncountable nouns with there is and there are.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

There are + plural noun

Decorations and guests are plural countable nouns. Use are.

A lot of + plural noun

Many and a lot of both express a large quantity with plural countable nouns. A lot of is especially common in everyday affirmative speech.

There is + uncountable noun

Food is uncountable in this general use. A lot of can come before it; many cannot.

There is some music.
There are some musicians.

One countable item

There is a gift on the table.

Several countable items

There are some gifts on the table.

A general amount

There is some food on the table.

Correct: There are many decorations. Avoid “There is many decorations” and “There are many food”. These are description patterns, rather than expressions of people’s future intentions.

03

Plans and Future Arrangements

Intentions, agreed arrangements, meaning contrasts and questions.

3.1

Celebration plans with going to

What we will learn: Understand an intention with be going to and connect it to the language introduced in Unit 3.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

An intention

subject + am/is/are + going to + base verb

The speaker presents a plan or intention. This sentence does not, by itself, tell us which practical arrangements are confirmed.

Other subjects and a negative

I am / he is / they are going to…
She isn’t going to travel.

An everyday plan

A fictional example of an intention related to a special occasion. For identifies the purpose or occasion of the trip.

Keep be and the base verb: She is going to travel, not “She going to travel” or “She is going to travelling”. This section applies going to to a new topic rather than introducing it again from the beginning.

3.2

Agreed future arrangements

What we will learn: Understand the present continuous when a speaker presents a future plan as organised.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

An organised meeting

The context is an agreed meeting. On Saturday at six places it in the future; the speaker presents it as arranged.

The same form, a future meaning

am/is/are + verb-ing

The second sentence supplies evidence of an arrangement. Travelling is not describing movement happening right now in this context.

A negative arrangement

Be + not makes the negative: am not, isn’t, aren’t. The sentences clarify the agreed location.

Happening now

We’re decorating the hall now.

Arranged for later

We’re decorating the hall on Friday at five. The volunteers have agreed on the time.

The verb form is the same as the continuous used in Units 2 and 3. The future meaning comes from context. A future date alone does not prove that an arrangement is confirmed.

3.3

Choosing the intended meaning

What we will learn: Distinguish four meanings and understand where future forms can overlap.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A custom

A repeated pattern: present simple.

An action now

An action in progress: present continuous.

An intention

A plan or intention: be going to + base verb.

An arrangement

In an agreed-meeting context, the speaker presents this as organised: present continuous.

Two ways to present a plan

We’re going to visit our cousins.
The speaker foregrounds an intention.

We’re visiting our cousins on Saturday.
In an agreed-visit context, the speaker presents an arrangement.

The forms can overlap

A confirmed plan can also be described with going to. A present continuous sentence does not need to list every booking or agreement.

Meaning comes from the situation and how the speaker presents the plan, not from a rigid “unconfirmed versus confirmed” rule.

The comparison connects familiar verb forms with their different uses. Time phrases and the wider situation help establish the meaning.

3.4

Questions and answers about plans

What we will learn: Recognise question order and short answers for intentions and arrangements.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Ask about an intention

Question word + be + subject + going to + base verb?

“We’re going to prepare dinner.”

Negative short answer: No, we aren’t.

Ask about an arrangement

Question word + be + subject + verb-ing?

“We’re meeting our cousins.”

Positive short answer: Yes, we are.

Ask for time and place

“On Saturday at six.”

“At the hotel.” Them refers to the cousins, connecting with the object pronouns from Unit 2.

The subject in an answer follows the situation: Are you…? can have Yes, I am for one person or Yes, we are for a group. Avoid “Do you are meeting…?”; be already forms the question.

We’re going to spend a few days in Valencia in March. We’re travelling on Friday, and our tickets are booked. On Saturday morning, we’re meeting our friend Ana at the hotel. She knows the city. How the plans are presented

We’re going to spend states an intention.
We’re travelling… tickets are booked presents an arrangement.
We’re meeting… at the hotel gives an arranged meeting.
I’m going to take my camera states another intention with a reason.

In written emails, the greeting, main message and closing normally appear on separate lines.

4.2

Useful expressions in context

What we will learn: Distinguish phrasal verbs and idioms and recognise their form, meaning and register.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Phrasal verb

preparar o disponer algo para usarlo

Arrange or prepare something so it is ready for use.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Set up the tables / set the tables up; with a pronoun: set them up.

Phrasal verb

colocar o colgar decoraciones

Place or hang something in position; here, decorations.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Put up the decorations / put the decorations up; with a pronoun: put them up.

Phrasal verb

reunirse

Meet and spend time with other people.

Use: Everyday, conversational. Get together with someone. The noun a get-together names a gathering.

Idiom

en pleno desarrollo o apogeo

Happening with a lot of activity.

Use: Everyday, conversational. Often follows be and describes an event in progress.

Idiom

pasarlo muy bien

Enjoy yourself very much.

Use: Informal, enthusiastic. It is not about a physical ball in this use.

The object pronoun comes between the parts of these separable verbs: put them up, not “put up them”. Have a good time is another useful expression for enjoyment; the idiom taught here is have a ball.
