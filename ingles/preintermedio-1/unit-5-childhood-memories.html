01

Childhood and Past Memories

Memory vocabulary, past states, completed actions and time expressions.

1.1

Childhood and memory words

What we will learn: Understand words for life stages, people, places and memories.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

The period of someone’s life when they are a child.

A person aged thirteen to nineteen.

A grown person, contrasted here with a child.

Something a person remembers from the past.

Someone in the same class.

An area of a town or city where people live.

A school for children in the early years of formal education.

The parents of someone’s parent.

A memory has a context

One of my favourite memories identifies a particular memory. Childhood places it in a period of life.

Remember and memory

Remember is a verb: “I remember my first school.”

A memory is a noun: “That is a happy memory.”

A memory description can concern family, friends, school or a fictional character.

1.2

Past descriptions with was and were

What we will learn: Recognise past states and descriptions and understand the fixed expression be born.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Past forms of be

I/he/she/it was
you/we/they were

Was and were connect a subject to a past description, location or state.

Negatives and questions

I wasn’t very talkative. My friends weren’t quiet.

Was she in the school band?
Yes, she was. / No, she wasn’t.

Be forms its own questions; there is no did here.

Where were you born?

Be born is the expression: “She was born in Colombia.” Avoid “I born” and “Where did you born?”

“I was shy at school” describes a past state. It does not, by itself, explain why someone felt that way or establish their personality now.

1.3

Completed actions in the past

What we will learn: Recognise regular and irregular past forms in a completed event or period.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

The past simple connects an action to a finished time or a clearly established past context.

live → lived

move → moved

play → played

visit → visited

study → studied

go → went

have → had

meet → met

A completed period

Until marks the end of that period. Was ten expresses the age within the past context.

An event

Moved is a completed action. In 2005 locates the event.

Regular endings in speech

Regular -ed does not always add a syllable. Played and lived end in /d/; visited has an extra /ɪd/ syllable.

A past-simple statement can also describe repeated actions in a finished period: “We visited our uncle every Sunday when I was a child.” A repeated meaning is not exclusive to used to.

1.4

Past questions, negatives and time

What we will learn: Understand did + base verb and distinguish dates, ages and periods of time.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Questions with action verbs

Did + subject + base verb?
Did you play in the school band?
Yes, I did. / No, I didn’t.

Use move after did, not moved.

A negative action

didn’t + base verb
She didn’t go, not “She didn’t went”.

Dates and ages

In 2005: a year.
At the age of ten / when I was ten: an age.
Two years ago: two years before now.
Until I was ten: the end of a period.

A complete fictional timeline for Mateo:

Born in Medellín.

→
2005

Moved from Bello to Envigado.

→
2013

Finished school.

→
Now

Lives in Envigado.

Compare Were you…? with Did you live…? The main verb determines the question pattern. The timeline is a completed example, not a personal-history task.

02

Past Habits with Used To

Earlier habits and states, questions, negatives and then-and-now meanings.

2.1

Past habits and states with used to

What we will learn: Understand used to + base verb for a past habit or state contrasted with the present.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A repeated action

The visits happened regularly in an earlier period. Used to commonly presents that habit as different from the present.

A past state

Live describes a past situation. The second clause makes the present contrast explicit.

One form for every subject

subject + used to + base verb
They used to be neighbours.

The affirmative form used to does not change with the subject.

Correct: She used to play. Avoid “She used to played” and “She used to playing”. In connected speech, used to often sounds like /ˈjuːstə/; the audio models show it inside complete sentences.

2.2

Questions and negatives with used to

What we will learn: Recognise the base spelling use to after did and didn’t.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A question

Did + subject + use to + base verb?
What games did you use to play?

The past marking is already carried by did.

A negative

subject + didn’t + use to + base verb

This describes the absence of a habit or state in that past period.

Short answers

Use did/didn’t in the short answer. The subject follows the person being discussed.

Affirmative

She used to live here.

Question

Did she use to live here?

Negative

She didn’t use to live here.

The standard written forms taught here are did you use to and didn’t use to. Avoid adding an -s to use or the main verb.

2.3

A past habit or one completed event?

What we will learn: Distinguish an earlier habit from a single event without treating the past simple as limited to one-off actions.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A past habit

A regular activity in an earlier period. The statement commonly implies a change.

One event

One completed occasion. Used to does not describe this single event.

The past simple can also express repetition

The time phrase and every Sunday make the repeated past meaning clear.

A useful choice of emphasis

We used to visit him every Sunday.
Highlights an earlier habit, commonly contrasted with now.

We visited him every Sunday that summer.
Reports repetition within a finished period.

A past description alone does not explain the cause of a change. A clear then-and-now account supplies the current situation rather than asking the reader to guess it.

2.4

Connecting then and now

What we will learn: Understand how but, now and still connect an earlier situation with the present.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

An explicit change

Used to have describes the earlier state. Now I have states the current one.

Use have long hair, not “take it long”.

A habit that continues

Still shows that the activity continues. This example states continuity directly.

Then and now refer to different times

Then: I lived near my grandparents.
Now: I live in a different neighbourhood.

The present simple describes the current situation. But introduces a contrast; and can add a continuing feature.

The used to construction in this unit concerns the past. Present habits use expressions such as usually: “I usually read after work.”

03

Changes and Comparisons

Appearance, personality in context, comparatives and an explained profile.

3.1

Describing appearance over time

What we will learn: Recognise be and have in descriptions of height and hair at two different times.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Hair with little length; hair is uncountable in this general use.

Hair with greater length.

Hair with curls.

Hair without curls in the description.

Having a greater height in the comparison.

Having a smaller height in the comparison.

Be + adjective

Was / am connect the person to a height description.

Have + hair description

Had describes the past; has describes the present.

Also possible: “Her hair was short then.”

A photograph can show features such as hair length in that picture. It does not establish someone’s habits, personality or life story. The comparisons here describe fictional profiles with stated information.

3.2

Personality with stated context

What we will learn: Understand descriptions of personality as a person’s own account in a particular situation.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Sometimes uncomfortable when meeting or speaking to people.

Comfortable meeting and talking to people.

Able to wait or help without becoming annoyed quickly.

Showing consideration for others in words and behaviour.

Not showing expected consideration in a situation.

A self-description

The speaker identifies the situation: in class and in small groups. This is a stated personal account.

A description with an example

The second sentence illustrates the description with a behaviour.

An appearance change does not prove an attitude change. A quieter child does not automatically become an outgoing adult. The examples report information supplied by the fictional speaker.

3.3

Comparatives for then and now

What we will learn: Recognise comparative forms and understand what is being compared.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Short adjective + -er

Long → longer.
Short → shorter.
Tall → taller.

Than introduces the other side of the comparison.

More + longer adjective

More outgoing · more patient · more confident.

Use more outgoing, not “outgoinger”.

Spelling and irregular forms

Happy → happier.
Good → better.
Bad → worse.

The form depends on the adjective; not every adjective takes more.

Correct

Longer than.
More patient than.
Better than.

Common corrections

“More longer” → longer.
“More better” → better.
“Longer that” → longer than.

A comparison can concern the same person at different times. “More outgoing now” is a comparison, not proof of why a person changed. These forms apply familiar comparison language to the unit topic.

3.4

A complete then-and-now profile

What we will learn: Notice how a stated profile combines past states, habits and a present comparison.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Mateo’s fictional profile supplies all the information used in this comparison.

Then

He lived in Bello.
He had short hair.
He played outside after school.
He described himself as quiet in class.

Now

He lives in Envigado.
His hair is longer.
He enjoys reading and walking.
He says he feels more confident in small groups.

Used to live presents a past state; had reports an earlier feature; longer compares two times; still identifies continuity. The account includes both changes and something that continues.

04

Language in Context

Narrative structure, complete memory models, expressions and the unit summary.

4.1

How a short memory is organised

What we will learn: Recognise the context, event sequence and ending of a short past narrative.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.
Context

When, where and who.

→
Events

What happened, in order.

→
Ending

What happened last and why the memory matters to the speaker.

An opening

When I was eight establishes a past time. The rest identifies the action and people.

A sequence

First, then, after that, finally organise events. Past verbs show that the actions belong to the past context.

An ending and a reason

Went closes the sequence. I remember is present because it describes remembering now.

Used to can establish the background habit. The past simple can then describe one particular occasion. Not every sentence in a memory needs used to.

A family memory

How the family memory works

  1. When I was a child introduces the past context.
  2. We used to visit establishes a repeated habit.
  3. One Sunday introduces one occasion.
  4. Took, arrived, helped, played, looked, went narrate completed actions.
  5. I remember… because explains the present meaning of that memory.

A school memory

In the school model, was describes the past situation; played, forgot, helped, finished, clapped narrate events; felt describes a past feeling. It is a memory returns to the speaker’s present view.

4.3

Expressions for remembering

What we will learn: Distinguish phrasal verbs and figurative expressions and recognise their meaning and use.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Phrasal verb

crecer o pasar la infancia

Develop from a child into an adult; with a place, describe where someone spent childhood.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Past form: grew up. Grow up in a place.

Phrasal verb

recordar o reflexionar sobre una etapa pasada

Think about something that happened in the past.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Keep on before the period or experience.

Phrasal verb

hacer que vuelvan recuerdos

Make someone remember a past feeling or experience; here, bring back memories.

Use: Everyday, neutral in this use. Bring back happy memories / bring happy memories back; with a pronoun: bring them back.

Idiomatic expression

los viejos tiempos recordados con nostalgia

An earlier period remembered fondly, often with nostalgia.

Use: Conversational. It expresses a positive view of the past, not proof that everything was better then.

Idiom

un recorrido por los recuerdos

An occasion for remembering experiences from the past.

Use: Conversational, figurative. It does not describe a literal road or journey.

Grow up describes growing or spending childhood somewhere. Look back on describes thinking about the past from the present. The same expressions are included in the expression library.

4.4

Unit summary

What we will learn: Recognise how past states, actions, habits and comparisons connect in the unit models.

  • I can understand childhood and memory vocabulary.
  • I can describe past states with was and were and understand be born.
  • I can recognise completed actions with regular and irregular past forms.
  • I can understand past questions and negatives with did + base verb.
  • I can describe earlier habits and states with used to.
  • I can distinguish used to in statements from use to after did and didn’t.
  • I can distinguish a past habit, a completed event and a continuing habit.
  • I can describe a stated change using present forms and comparatives.
  • I can understand the structure of a short family or school memory.
