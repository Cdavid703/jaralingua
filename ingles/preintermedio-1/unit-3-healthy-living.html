01

Healthy Habits and Eating

Everyday habits, frequency, temporary changes and food-label language.

1.1

Everyday habits

What we will learn: Understand words and expressions for food, activity and rest in everyday routines.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Drink is the action; water is the thing you drink.

Eat the first meal of the day. Have is common with meal names.

Prepare food in your own home.

Walk for a while, often for enjoyment.

Do physical activity. As a general activity, exercise is uncountable.

Stop an activity for a short time.

Stop working or being active for a while.

Sleep for the amount the speaker needs. Enough means sufficient.

A routine

The present simple describes a regular pattern. After work tells us when the walk happens.

Words that belong together

have breakfast · drink water · take a break · get enough sleep

These combinations are useful language chunks. “Take a break” names a short pause; “break” alone can have other meanings.

Healthy living is the unit topic. The examples show how people describe everyday habits and choices in English.

1.2

How often: frequency adverbs

What we will learn: Recognise frequency and the usual position of an adverb with a main verb or be.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

On every occasion in the situation described.

On most occasions.

On many occasions.

On some occasions.

Almost never.

On no occasion in the situation described.

Before a main verb

subject + frequency adverb + main verb

She often walks to work. The adverb tells us how often the action happens.

After be

subject + am/is/are + frequency adverb

He is usually calm. Here, be connects the subject to a description.

Never already has a negative meaning

Use I never skip, not “I don’t never skip”. Skip breakfast means not having that meal.

The scale shows an approximate order, not fixed percentages. Every day and twice a week are frequency phrases, usually placed at the end: “I walk twice a week.”

1.3

Routines and temporary changes

What we will learn: Distinguish a regular pattern from an action or temporary change happening around now.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A regular pattern

Present simple: a routine.
She brings lunch. She doesn’t buy lunch every day.
Does she bring lunch? Yes, she does.

A temporary change

Present continuous: a change around now.
She is buying lunch this week. She isn’t cooking at home this week.
Is she buying lunch? Yes, she is.

Routine → do/does

“I usually eat rice and vegetables.”

Around now → am/is/are

“I’m trying different lunches this week.”

This week can include times when the action is not happening at the exact moment of speaking. Time expressions help establish context, but the speaker’s intended meaning determines the tense. This develops the contrast introduced in Unit 2.

1.4

Understanding a food label

What we will learn: Read serving size, amounts and units in an illustrated label.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Serving and package

A serving is the portion used for the listed values. A bag or package can contain more than one serving.

Amounts and units

g = grams; kcal = kilocalories, a unit of energy.

The column matters: the example shows 3 g per serving and 6 g per bag.

Language for quantities

How much sugar is there in one serving?
There are three grams.

How many servings are there in the bag?
There are two servings.

Sugar names a substance; servings are countable portions.

These invented values illustrate English on a label. The two columns describe different amounts of the same product; they are not two different foods.

02

Aches, Pains and Recommendations

Feelings, advice, suggestions, obligation and habitual situations.

2.1

Describing how you feel

What we will learn: Distinguish have + a noun from feel/be + an adjective.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Pain in the head.

Pain in the stomach area.

A throat that hurts.

The noun for repeated coughing.

A common illness; different from feeling cold.

Feeling a need to rest.

Feeling pressure or worry.

Feeling calm and at ease.

Have + noun

I have a cold. She has a sore throat.
In these examples, the noun takes a.

Feel / be + adjective

She feels stressed. She is stressed.
Use “I’m tired”, not “I have tired”.

Two different meanings

Have a cold refers to an illness.
Feel cold describes temperature sensation.

The question How do you feel? asks about a current feeling or condition. “I feel tired” reports a feeling; it does not name its cause.

2.2

Advice with should

What we will learn: Understand advice, negative advice and questions using should + base verb.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

A recommendation

subject + should + base verb

Should presents something as a good idea. The form stays the same: I should talk; she should talk.

Negative advice

subject + shouldn’t + base verb

Shouldn’t = should not. It advises against an action.

Ask for advice

Should + subject + base verb?

No, you shouldn’t. A full response can explain a reason.

Correct: She should rest.
Not: She should rests.
Not: She should to rest.

Should takes the base verb. Questions do not use do/does: Should she rest?

2.3

Suggestions with could

What we will learn: Recognise could as a way to offer an option rather than an obligation.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

One possible option

subject + could + base verb

Could presents an available idea. Another option may also be possible.

More than one option

Or connects alternatives. The speaker is offering suggestions.

Compare the purpose

Should recommends a course of action.
Could suggests one way to do it.

Could has other uses in English. Here, it introduces a suggestion. It is not describing an ability in the past. Like should, it takes a base verb without to or a third-person s.

2.4

Obligation and necessity

What we will learn: Distinguish must, have to, mustn’t and don’t have to in clearly stated situations.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Must

must + base verb expresses a strong requirement. It can communicate a rule or the speaker’s insistence.

Have to / has to

have/has to + base verb expresses necessity or obligation, often because of circumstances or rules.

Does she have to start at nine?
Yes, she does. Use have after does.

Prohibition

Mustn’t = must not: the action is not allowed by the stated rule.

No obligation

Don’t/doesn’t have to: the action is optional. It is not prohibited.

Same necessity, different constructions

She must arrive on time.
She has to arrive on time.

Both can express necessity. The context matters; must is not always “personal” and have to is not always “external”.

Avoid changing the meaning

She doesn’t have to come.
Coming is optional.

She mustn’t come.
Coming is forbidden in the situation.

The library and copy requirements are fictional contexts for comparing meanings. The examples do not set rules for this course.

2.5

Habitual situations with if and when

What we will learn: Understand a condition or situation and its usual result using present forms.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

When: a situation that happens

The speaker describes what normally happens in that situation. Both verbs are in the present simple.

If: a condition

If introduces a condition. The main clause describes the speaker’s usual response when that condition is met.

Third-person forms still matter

She feels; she talks. If and when do not remove the usual present-simple verb endings.

Situation

When I feel tired,

→
Usual response

I take a break.

The order can change

When I feel tired, I take a break.
I take a break when I feel tired.

A comma normally follows an introductory if/when clause. It is usually unnecessary when that clause comes last.

A question about a usual response

“I usually talk to a friend.” The question asks about a repeated response.

This section covers habitual meanings, often called the zero conditional with if. It does not introduce hypothetical situations or future predictions.

03

Stress and Relaxation

Daily demands, time questions, suggestions and personal intentions.

3.1

A demanding day

What we will learn: Understand words for daily demands, feelings, pauses and rest.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Having many things to do.

The time by which something must be finished.

Demands that make a situation feel difficult.

Time outside work and other responsibilities.

Become less tense or spend time at ease.

A regular way of doing things.

Describe demands and feelings

Have + noun: I have a deadline.
Feel + adjective: I feel stressed.

Explain a reason

Because introduces the reason. This week supports a temporary situation here.

Stressed describes how someone feels; stressful describes something that causes that feeling. “I feel stressed” and “This is a stressful week” have different subjects and meanings.

3.2

Hours, duration and frequency

What we will learn: Recognise whether a question asks for a number of hours, a duration or a frequency.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

How many hours…?

“About seven hours.” Hours is a plural countable noun. This asks for a number of hours, not a number of occasions.

How often…?

“Three times a week.” This asks about frequency.

How long…?

“Thirty minutes.” This asks about duration; the answer can use minutes or hours.

Number of hours: How many hours do you work a day?
I work eight hours a day.

Frequency: How often do you work late?
I sometimes work late.

The numbers are fictional answers that illustrate question meanings. “How much hours” is incorrect: use How many hours. After does, use last, not lasts.

3.3

You could try…

What we will learn: Understand the two verb patterns inside a suggestion with could try.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.
Could + base verb

You could try

→
Try + activity in -ing

taking a short break.

Suggest an activity as an option

Could try introduces the suggestion. Taking names the activity to try.

Another complete example

The clause before you start places the suggested activity in time.

Try doing and try to do

Try doing something can mean testing an approach to see whether it helps.

Try to do something means making an effort to do it.

Both patterns exist. This section focuses on suggestions with try + -ing.

Correct: You could try taking… Avoid “You could to try…” and “You could tries…”. The -ing form here names an activity; it is not a present continuous tense.

3.4

Personal intentions with going to

What we will learn: Recognise an intention and form statements, negatives and questions with be going to.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

An intention

subject + am/is/are + going to + base verb

The speaker has an intention. I’m supplies am; the main action is organise.

Other subjects and a negative

She isn’t going to work late. They aren’t going to stay.

Questions and short answers

Am/Is/Are + subject + going to + base verb?

What are you going to change?
I’m going to finish earlier.

Habit

I usually make a list.

Temporary change

I’m making a list this week.

Intention

I’m going to make a list tonight.

Keep both parts: I am going to rest, not “I going to rest”. Use a base verb after to: going to rest, not “going to resting”. These examples concern intentions, rather than physical movement to a place.

4.2

Useful expressions in context

What we will learn: Distinguish phrasal verbs from idioms and recognise their meaning, form and register.

SpeedTap a word or sentence to listen.

Phrasal verb

reducir la cantidad o frecuencia de algo

Do or use less of something.

Use: Everyday, neutral. Keep on before the noun or activity.

Phrasal verb

dejar de hacer algo

Stop an activity or habit.

Use: Everyday, neutral in this use. Follow it with a noun or an -ing activity; with an object pronoun: give it up.

Phrasal verb

relajarse después de un periodo de actividad

Gradually relax after being busy.

Use: Everyday, conversational. Here wind is pronounced like the verb in wind a clock.

Idiom

sentirse un poco indispuesto

Feel slightly unwell.

Use: Informal, common. It describes a feeling without naming a specific illness.

Idiom

recuperar energías o descansar

Rest and feel ready for activity again.

Use: Conversational, figurative. Change your to my, her or our to match the person.

Take a break is a useful everyday combination, not a phrasal verb. Recharge your batteries is figurative here; no physical batteries are involved. The same five expressions appear in the expression library.
