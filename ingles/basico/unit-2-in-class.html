02

Verb To Be in the Classroom

In Unit 2, the verb to be helps students identify objects, locate people and things, and build classroom questions.

Identification

Use be to say what something is.

This is a pencil. That is the teacher. These are notebooks.

Location

Use be before location information.

The book is on the desk. The keys are in the bag. The students are in class.

Possession

Use be to connect an object and an owner.

This is my notebook. Those are Ana's keys. This pencil is mine.

Questions

Where is the backpack?It is under the chair.
Are these your earbuds?Yes, they are. / No, they are not.
Whose pencil is this?It is Laura's pencil.

Negatives

The phone is not on the desk.Use is not for one object.
These are not my keys.Use are not for plural objects.
We are not in the lab.Use are not with we.

03

Location: In, On, and At

These prepositions help students connect people and objects with places. They are small words, but they control meaning.

Real context: where things are

Students need location words to say where their backpack, notebook, phone, keys, and classmates are in the classroom.

The backpack is under the chair. The notebook is on the desk.

In

Use in when something is inside a place, container, or general area.

in the classroomin the backpackin the bag

On

Use on when something touches a surface.

on the deskon the chairon the board

At

Use at for a point, institution, activity location, or specific place.

at schoolat the doorat the teacher's desk

05

This, That, These, and Those

Demonstratives help students point to people and objects. The correct word depends on distance and number.

Real context: near and far

When an object is close to the speaker, use this or these. When it is far, use that or those.

06

Possession and Whose

Students need possession language to say which object belongs to which person.

Real context: whose object is it?

In class, students often need to identify an object and its owner: a notebook, a phone, keys, earbuds, a pencil, or a backpack.

Whose notebook is this? It is Ana's notebook.

Possessive adjectives

my notebookIt belongs to me.
your pencilIt belongs to you.
his / her phoneIt belongs to him / her.

Owner + 's

Ana's notebookel cuaderno de Ana
Daniel's backpackla mochila de Daniel
Laura's keyslas llaves de Laura

Possessive pronouns

Use these when the object is already clear.

mine / yours / his / hersThis pencil is mine.

With whose

Whose bag is this?It is my bag.
Whose keys are these?They are Carlos's keys.

Who vs. Whose

Who is that?Ask for a person.
Whose phone is this?Ask for an owner.
Key grammar focus

Whose = de quién / de quiénes

Whose is a WH question word for possession. It does not ask for a person directly; it asks for the owner of an object. This is difficult for many Spanish speakers because Spanish uses de quién, but English does not say of who in this basic classroom pattern.

UseStructureClassroom exampleAnswer options
One objectWhose + singular noun + is + this/that?Whose pencil is this?It is Ana's pencil. / It is Ana's. / It is hers.
Two or more objectsWhose + plural noun + are + these/those?Whose keys are these?They are Carlos's keys. / They are Carlos's. / They are his.
Object + locationWhose + noun + is/are + location phrase?Whose notebook is on the desk?It is Laura's notebook. / It is Laura's.

Full answer

Use the owner's name and the object when the situation is not clear yet.

It is Ana's notebook.

Short answer

Use the owner's name + 's when the object is already clear.

It is Ana's.

Pronoun answer

Use a possessive pronoun when the class already knows the owner.

It is hers. / They are his.

Why not who?

Who is that?Pregunta por una persona: ¿Quién es?
Whose pencil is this?Pregunta por el dueño: ¿De quién es este lápiz?
Who has the pencil?Pregunta quién tiene el lápiz ahora, not necessarily the owner.

Common Spanish-speaker mistakes

Who pencil is this? → Whose pencil is this?Use whose before the object.
Of who is this pencil? → Whose pencil is this?Do not translate de quién word by word.
Who are these keys? → Whose keys are these?Keys are things, not people. Ask for the owner.

Who

Use who when the answer is a person.

Who is that? That is the teacher.

Whose

Use whose when the answer is an owner.

Whose bag is this? It is Maria's.

Who's

Who's sounds like whose, but it means who is.

Who's that? = Who is that?
Study note: the corrections show sentence patterns students must notice before speaking or writing classroom descriptions. In this section, students must especially separate who for people, whose for possession, and who's for who is.
08

Classroom Language

Classroom language gives students the phrases they need to participate in class from the beginning of the course.

Teacher instructions

Open your book.Start using the book.
Close your notebook.Stop using the notebook.
Look at the board.Direct attention to the board.

Class participation

Listen, please.Pay attention.
Repeat, please.Say it again.
Work with a partner.Collaborate with another student.

Student requests

Can you repeat, please?Ask for repetition.
I don't understand.Say you need help.
How do you say ___ in English?Ask for a word.
