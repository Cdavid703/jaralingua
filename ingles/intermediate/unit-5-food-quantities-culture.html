Grammar 1

Countable and uncountable food nouns

Students must know whether they are counting individual items or naming a substance, category, or ingredient.

+

Meaning

Countable nouns are individual items. They can be singular or plural. Uncountable nouns are substances, liquids, grains, or general food categories. They usually do not take plural -s.

countable: a tomato / two tomatoes / an egg / six eggs
uncountable: some rice / a little oil / much sugar / no bread
We need two avocados. You can count the avocados one by one.
We need some rice. Rice is treated as a substance or grain.
We need three cups of rice. The container makes the amount countable.

Use this image for

Ingredient identification: countable items, uncountable ingredients, and recipe quantities.

Countable item

an egg, two eggs, a tomato, three tomatoes, an avocado, two avocados

Uncountable ingredient

rice, water, oil, yogurt, cheese, bread, chicken as meat

Context change

chicken can mean meat. a chicken can mean one animal or a whole chicken.

Common mistake

Incorrect: We need three rices.
Correct: We need three cups of rice.

Common mistake

Incorrect: There are many cheese in the salad.
Correct: There is a lot of cheese in the salad. / There are many slices of cheese.

Useful teacher note

If students can point to separate pieces, the noun is often countable. If they scoop, pour, spread, or measure it, the noun is often uncountable.

Grammar 2

Quantifiers: some, any, much, many, a few, a little

Quantifiers help students say what they have, what is missing, and whether the amount is healthy or excessive.

+

some / any / no

Use some in affirmative sentences. Use any in questions and negatives. Use no when the amount is zero.

We have some tomatoes.
Do we have any lettuce?
We have no bread.

many / much / a lot of

Use many with countable plural nouns. Use much with uncountable nouns, especially in questions and negatives. A lot of works with both.

How many tomatoes do we need?
How much rice do we need?
We need a lot of vegetables.

a few / a little

Use a few with countable plural nouns. Use a little with uncountable nouns.

a few strawberries
a few eggs
a little yogurt

enough

Enough means the amount is sufficient for the purpose.

We have enough rice for six people.
There are enough vegetables for the salad.

too much / too many

Use these when the amount is excessive.

There is too much oil in the dressing.
There are too many desserts on the menu.

Common mistake

Incorrect: How many rice do we need?
Correct: How much rice do we need? / How many cups of rice do we need?
Grammar 3

Containers, measures, and partitives

When a food is uncountable, students need a container or partitive phrase to give a clear amount.

+
Containera bottle of water

Use containers for liquids and packaged food.

Measurea cup of rice

Use measures for recipes and serving plans.

Piecea piece of chicken

Use pieces for portions or cut food.

Shapea slice of cheese

Use shape words when food is cut or served that way.

Servinga bowl of soup

Use serving nouns when describing meals.

Packagea can of beans

Use package nouns when shopping or planning.

Sentence building path

Food: rice
Quantity phrase: three cups of rice
Sentence: We need three cups of rice for six people.

This path helps students move from vocabulary to usable English.

Common mistake

Incorrect: I bought two waters and three breads.
Correct: I bought two bottles of water and three loaves of bread.
Grammar 4

made of, made from, and made with

These phrases explain ingredients and preparation, but they do not mean exactly the same thing.

+

made of

Use it when the materials or ingredients are visible or easy to identify.

The salad is made of lettuce, tomato, corn, and avocado.
The bowl is made of rice, beans, chicken, and vegetables.

made from

Use it when one ingredient changes into a different product.

Cheese is made from milk.
Yogurt is made from milk.

made with

Use it to introduce recipe ingredients naturally.

The dressing is made with lime, oil, salt, and cilantro.
The dessert is made with mango, strawberries, and a little yogurt.

Common mistake

Incorrect: The salad is made by lettuce.
Correct: The salad is made of lettuce. / The salad is made with lettuce.

Recipe model

This rice bowl is made with cooked rice, beans, grilled chicken, lettuce, tomato, corn, avocado, and lime dressing. It is inspired by Colombian home food but presented as a modern healthy bowl.

Communication

Food adjectives with evidence

Intermediate students need to support opinions with taste, texture, health, and reasons.

+

Taste

sweetsaltyspicysourmild

Use taste words to describe what the food is like in the mouth.

Texture

crispycreamysoftcrunchysmooth

Texture helps reviews sound more specific.

Health and portion

balancedfillinglightfreshheavy

Health adjectives should be explained with ingredients or portions.

Weak opinion

The food is nice and good.

Stronger opinion

The dish is fresh and filling because it has vegetables, beans, rice, and avocado.

Review model

I would give it 4 out of 5 because it is crispy outside, soft inside, and not too heavy. It is simple, affordable, and easy to share.

Communication

Cultural comparison without stereotypes

Students should compare dishes through ingredients, occasions, preparation, and meaning.

+

Respectful comparison path

  1. Name the dish: This dish is called...
  2. Name ingredients: It is made with...
  3. Name the situation: People often eat it when...
  4. Compare carefully: It is similar to..., but it is different because...
  5. Explain meaning: It reminds people of...

Use this image for

Snack review, ratings, ingredient evidence, and respectful cultural comparison.

similar to

This rice bowl is similar to other global bowls because it combines grains, protein, vegetables, and sauce.

different from

It is different from a traditional dish because it changes the presentation and portion size.

reminds me of

It reminds me of Colombian home food because it uses rice, beans, avocado, and lime.

usually served with

It can be served with lime dressing and fruit salad.

Avoid shallow culture

Colombian food is always rice and beans.

Better

This menu is inspired by Colombian home food because it uses familiar ingredients, but it is only one possible version.
Grammar 5

Context changes: when food words change meaning

Some food nouns can be countable or uncountable depending on what the speaker means.

+

chicken

Chicken usually means meat. A chicken means one animal or one whole chicken.

We need some chicken for the bowl.
My grandmother bought a chicken for Sunday lunch.

coffee

Coffee is the drink in general. A coffee means one cup or one serving.

I drink coffee after lunch.
Can I have a coffee, please?

cake

Cake can mean the food in general. A cake means one whole cake.

I do not eat much cake.
She made a cake for the food fair.

fish

Fish can mean food. A fish means one animal. In food contexts, the uncountable meaning is common.

This dish is made with fish and lime.
There is a fish in the picture.

Meaning shift

These changes are not random. English often changes the noun when we move from food as material to one item or serving.

some chicken describes chicken as food.
a chicken describes one whole chicken or one animal.

Common mistake

Incorrect: I need two chickens for my salad.
Correct: I need two pieces of chicken for my salad.
Vocabulary for explanation

Recipe language for clear instructions

Recipe verbs help students describe preparation without turning the page into a cooking activity.

+

Preparation verbs

addmixslicechopboilgrillpourseasonserve

These verbs make ingredient descriptions more precise.

Quantity + verb

Add a little oil to the dressing.
Slice two tomatoes.
Pour a cup of water into the pot.
Serve it with avocado and lime.

Preparation vs. ingredients

Ingredients say what is in the dish. Preparation verbs say what happens to those ingredients.

The dressing is made with lime, oil, salt, and cilantro.
Mix the lime juice with a little oil, salt, and cilantro.

Common mistake

Incorrect: Put a rice in the bowl.
Correct: Put some rice in the bowl. / Put one cup of rice in the bowl.

Meaning notes

Season means adding salt, herbs, spices, or flavor. Slice means cutting food into thin pieces. Pour is used for liquids. Serve with names the food that accompanies the dish.

Model paragraph

This Colombian-inspired rice bowl is made with cooked rice, beans, grilled chicken, lettuce, tomato, corn, avocado, and lime dressing. For six people, we need three cups of cooked rice, two cans of beans, six pieces of chicken, some vegetables, and a little oil. The dish is balanced because it has grains, protein, vegetables, and healthy fat. It reminds me of Colombian home food, but it is served like a modern bowl. I would recommend it because it is filling, colorful, and easy to share.

Why the model works

Dish and ingredientsIt names the dish and explains what it is made with.
QuantitiesIt includes exact amounts for a group.
Health reasonIt says why the dish is balanced.
CultureIt compares tradition and modern presentation respectfully.
RecommendationIt gives a clear opinion with evidence.

Common mistake

Incorrect: I recommend eat it.
Correct: I recommend eating it. / I would recommend it.

Common mistake

Incorrect: It remembers me of my grandmother's food.
Correct: It reminds me of my grandmother's food.
Natural English

Phrasal verbs and idioms for food, taste, and healthy choices

Use a small set of natural expressions that match this unit: planning meals, eating habits, taste, and recommendations.

+

Why this belongs in Unit 5

Food conversations often sound flat when students only use nouns. These expressions let them talk about eating outside, reducing sugar, testing a dish, adding variety, and personal taste.

They are explanation language and model language. Deeper practice should stay in a separate workshop.

Figurative meaning

not my cup of tea is figurative. It does not literally mean tea. bring something to the table can mean contribute an idea, skill, or value, not only bring food.

Phrasal verbs

eat outcomer fuera / en restaurante

Meaning: to eat in a restaurant or outside the home.

  • We usually eat out on Fridays, but this week we are cooking at home.
  • Eating out can be expensive if we do it every day.
cut back onreducir / consumir menos

Meaning: to reduce the amount of something, especially for health.

  • We should cut back on sugar in the dessert.
  • Mateo wants to cut back on fried food.
try outprobar / ensayar

Meaning: to test something new to see if it works or tastes good.

  • Let's try out a lime dressing for the rice bowl.
  • The class can try out a small sample before reviewing it.
fill up onllenarse con / saciarse con

Meaning: to eat enough of something so you feel full.

  • Do not fill up on bread before the main dish.
  • Beans and rice can help you fill up on healthier food.

Idioms and fixed expressions

not my cup of teano es lo mio / no me gusta mucho

Meaning: something is not your preference.

  • Very spicy food is not my cup of tea, but I can still describe it respectfully.
  • Sweet snacks are not Ana's cup of tea.
have a sweet toothgustarle mucho lo dulce

Meaning: to like sweet food very much.

  • Nina has a sweet tooth, so she suggests fruit salad.
  • I have a sweet tooth, but I am cutting back on sugar.
bring something to the tableaportar algo valioso

Meaning: to contribute an idea, skill, ingredient, or value.

  • Avocado brings healthy fat to the table.
  • Each student should bring one useful idea to the table.
spice things updarle mas sabor / hacerlo mas interesante

Meaning: to make something more exciting, flavorful, or interesting.

  • A little chili can spice things up.
  • A cultural comparison can spice up a food review.

Common mistake

Incorrect: I have sweet tooth.
Correct: I have a sweet tooth.

Common mistake

Incorrect: This food is not my tea cup.
Correct: This food is not my cup of tea.

Common mistake

Incorrect: We need to cut back sugar.
Correct: We need to cut back on sugar.
