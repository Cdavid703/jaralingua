01 Wish, hope, dream, or goal?Choose the word by possibility, reality, emotional importance, and planning. +
The central distinction

These words all express desire, but they place that desire in a different relationship with reality.

A hope is normally possible. A dream is an important aspiration. A wish often contrasts with present or past reality. A goal is a result that can be organized into steps. Ask what is true now, how possible the result is, and whether the speaker has a plan.

hope

Meaning: something possible that you want to happen.

I hope I get the scholarship.

dream

Meaning: a meaningful ambition, often emotional or long-term.

I dream of managing a community project.

wish

Meaning: a desire that contrasts with reality or cannot now be changed.

I wish I had more experience.

goal

Meaning: a specific result you intend to work toward.

My goal is to complete the program this year.

would like

Meaning: a neutral, polite desire without claiming it is unreal.

I would like to study abroad next year.

make a wish

Meaning: use wish as a noun for a special desire.

Close your eyes and make a wish.

Form choices

Build each idea with its natural pattern

Possible future

hope + clause

hope + subject + present / future form

I hope the interview goes well.
I hope they will consider my proposal.

Same subject

hope to + verb

The person who hopes also performs the action.

We hope to finish by Friday.

Aspiration

dream of/about + -ing

Use a noun or an -ing form after of or about.

She dreams of opening a studio.

Other uses of wish

Not every use of wish describes an unreal situation

Social expression

I wish you luck in the interview.

Here, wish sends a positive message to another person.

Formal intention

I wish to speak with the program coordinator.

This formal use means want to. It is not the counterfactual grammar taught in the next sections.

Dream + infinitive

I dream to open a business.

I dream of opening a business.

After dream of, use a noun or an -ing form.

Hope for an unreal present

I hope I had more time now.

I wish I had more time now.

The phrase contrasts with present reality, so wish is the natural choice.
Meaning modelHear one situation expressed as a hope, dream, wish, and goal.
02 Present wishesUse a past form to show that present reality is different. +
Time versus distance

The past form after wish does not refer to past time.

It creates grammatical distance from reality. I wish I lived closer means that I do not live close now. The wish concerns the present, even though lived looks like a past-tense verb.

Core form

wish + subject + past simple

subject + wish(es) + subject + past form

I wish I had more time.
Renata wishes she lived closer.

Read the hidden reality

The sentence implies the opposite

I wish I knew the answer.

Present reality: I do not know the answer.

He wishes the course was not so expensive.

Present reality: the course is expensive.

Special verb

Were is the safest standard form with be

Recommended for the course

I wish I were...

Were clearly signals a hypothetical meaning and works in academic, professional, and careful speech.

I wish I were more confident.

Common in conversation

I wish I was...

Was is widespread in informal conversation with I, he, she, and it. It is not a different meaning.

She wishes she was closer to campus.

Reliable choice: use were in assessments and in the fixed advice pattern If I were you....

Present form after wish

I wish I have more confidence.

I wish I had more confidence.

The past form signals that the present reality is different.

Confusing wish with hope

I wish the meeting goes well tomorrow.

I hope the meeting goes well tomorrow.

The meeting is a real future possibility, not a contrary present reality.
Present-wish modelListen for the wish and the opposite present reality hidden behind it.
03 Wishes about ability or possibilityUse could when an ability, opportunity, or condition is unavailable. +
Unavailable now

Wish + could expresses an ability or possibility that reality does not currently allow

The subject after wish may be the speaker or another person. After could, always use the base form of the verb.

Ability

wish + could + verb

I wish I could speak more confidently.

Reality: I cannot yet speak as confidently as I want.

External possibility

A circumstance prevents the action

We wish we could attend the conference.

Reality: money, time, distance, or another condition prevents attendance.

Meaning contrast

Choose between reality, possibility, and an unavailable situation

Current ability

I can attend the workshop.

The ability or possibility exists now.

Desired but unavailable

I wish I could attend the workshop.

The speaker cannot attend.

Possible future

I hope I can attend next month.

The future opportunity is still possible.

More direct reality

I cannot attend because I work Saturdays.

This reports the fact without expressing a wish.

Can after wish

I wish I can travel this year.

I wish I could travel this year.

Use could to mark the unavailable possibility.

Infinitive after could

I wish I could to travel.

I wish I could travel.

Modal verbs take the base verb without to.
Ability modelHear the difference between can, hope I can, and wish I could.
04 Future wishes and complaintsUse would for a desired change, especially when you do not control it. +
Desired change

Wish + would focuses on a change in behavior or in a frustrating situation.

It often sounds like a complaint because the speaker wants someone or something to behave differently. The person making the wish usually does not control that change.

Behavior

wish + subject + would + verb

I wish my coworker would answer my messages.

The coworker's repeated behavior frustrates the speaker.

Situation

A change outside your control

We wish the rain would stop.

The speaker wants the situation to change but cannot directly change it.

Wish or hope?

Use hope for a neutral future possibility

Neutral future

I hope the bus arrives on time.

The speaker sees an ordinary future possibility.

Repeated frustration

I wish the bus would arrive on time for once.

The speaker is dissatisfied with a repeated problem.

Control check

Do not use wish I would for your own ordinary decision

Not normally: I wish I would study more.

Better: I should study more. / I hope I can develop a better study routine.

If the speaker controls the action, describe the intention, responsibility, or hoped-for improvement directly.

Will after wish

I wish the interview will go well.

I hope the interview will go well.

A real future possibility normally takes hope.

Complaint without context

I wish you would call me.

I wish you would call me when the schedule changes.

Context helps the listener understand the desired behavior and reduces unnecessary blame.
Future-wish modelHear how tone changes a neutral hope into a request for change.
05 Past wishes and regretsCompare imagining a different past, naming regret, and evaluating your decision. +
A past that cannot change

Use the past perfect after wish to imagine a different result before now.

I wish I had accepted the offer means that I did not accept it, and that decision is now finished. The past perfect places the unreal alternative before the speaker's present reflection.

Different past

wish + had + past participle

I wish I had asked for advice.

Fact: I did not ask.

Name the regret

regret + noun / -ing

I regret that decision.
I regret leaving early.

Evaluate yourself

should have + participle

I should have compared the options.

This identifies a better past action.

Same event, different focus

Choose the structure by what you want to communicate

  1. 1
    I regret turning down the internship.

    The speaker directly names the regretted action.

  2. 2
    I wish I had accepted the internship.

    The speaker imagines the opposite past.

  3. 3
    I should have asked more questions first.

    The speaker evaluates what the better action was.

  4. 4
    If I could go back, I would ask for more information.

    The speaker imagines how they would act differently.

Formal exception

Regret to + verb introduces bad news

We regret to inform you that the program is full.

This formal phrase means that the speaker is sorry to give the information. It does not describe a personal past action that the speaker wishes to undo.

Past simple for past regret

I wish I accepted the offer last year.

I wish I had accepted the offer last year.

Use the past perfect for a finished past that cannot change.

Have after should

I should of asked.

I should have asked.

Should've may sound like “should of,” but the written form is always should have.
Regret modelHear the same missed opportunity expressed with regret, wish, and should have.
06 Second conditionalConnect an imagined present or future condition with its possible result. +
Imagined situation

The second conditional is about distance from reality, not about past time.

Use it when a present or future situation is imaginary, unlikely, or being explored only as a possibility. The past form marks the hypothetical condition; would, could, or might introduces the result.

Core form

If + past, would + base verb

If + subject + past form, subject + would + base verb

If I received the scholarship, I would move.

Question

What would you do if...?

What would + subject + do + if + past form?

What would you do if you found a wallet on campus?

Result meaning

Would, could, and might do different jobs

would

A predicted or chosen result.

I would report it.

could

An available ability or option.

I could contact security.

might

A less certain possibility.

The owner might return for it.

Form details

Clause order changes punctuation, not meaning

If-clause first

If I had more time, I would volunteer.

Use a comma after the opening condition.

Result first

I would volunteer if I had more time.

A comma is normally unnecessary when the condition comes second.

Be and hypothetical meaning

Use were as the reliable standard option

If I were the program coordinator, I would extend the deadline.

Was is common in informal conversation with singular subjects, but were clearly marks the hypothetical meaning and is recommended for the course.

Would inside the if-clause

If I would have time, I would help.

If I had time, I would help.

In the standard pattern, the if-clause takes a past form, not would.

To after would

I would to change the policy.

I would change the policy.

Use the base verb directly after a modal.
Conditional modelHear one imagined condition with would, could, and might results.
07 Understanding and discussing dilemmasMove from a quick opinion to a reasoned decision with consequences. +
More than a problem

A dilemma is a difficult choice in which every option has a cost, risk, or competing value.

A problem may have a clear solution. A choice simply offers alternatives. A dilemma forces a person to compare consequences or values such as honesty, loyalty, fairness, privacy, and responsibility.

Problem

A difficulty to solve

The registration system is not working.

The goal is to find a practical solution.

Choice

Alternatives are available

I can take a morning or evening class.

Neither option necessarily creates a serious conflict.

Dilemma

Important values compete

Should I report a friend's dishonest work if reporting it could end our friendship?

Honesty and loyalty pull in different directions.

Accessible distinction

Moral and ethical perspectives can overlap

Moral perspective

Personal or cultural beliefs about right and wrong.

I believe honesty is important, even when the truth is uncomfortable.

Ethical perspective

Principles, duties, or professional standards used by a group or institution.

A counselor must protect private student information.

Avoid absolute judgments: different people may prioritize different values. A strong answer explains the reasoning and likely consequences.
Reasoning route

Six steps for a clear opinion

  1. 1
    Identify the facts.

    Separate what is known from assumptions.

  2. 2
    Identify the people affected.

    Ask who benefits, who could be harmed, and who has responsibility.

  3. 3
    Name the options.

    Include realistic alternatives and possible compromises.

  4. 4
    Predict the consequences.

    Use conditionals: If I chose this option, it might...

  5. 5
    Identify the values.

    Consider honesty, fairness, loyalty, safety, privacy, and responsibility.

  6. 6
    State and justify a decision.

    I would probably... because...

In my view...

Introduce your position without presenting it as the only possible answer.

It depends on...

Show that missing facts or conditions affect the decision.

The fairest option would be...

Connect your recommendation to fairness.

I would probably...

State a likely imaginary decision without false certainty.

One possible consequence is...

Predict an outcome before deciding.

I understand both sides, but...

Recognize the competing reasons before giving your position.

Dilemma modelHear a reasoned opinion that identifies options, values, and consequences without moralizing.
08 Giving advice at the right strengthChoose a gentle option, clear recommendation, personal perspective, or warning. +
Respectful advice

Good advice matches the urgency of the situation and leaves the listener room to decide.

Could offers an option. Should recommends. If I were you makes the advice personal but hypothetical. Had better warns about a likely negative consequence and therefore sounds stronger.

  1. 1
    You could talk to the academic advisor.

    A low-pressure option; other choices remain open.

  2. 2
    You might want to compare the deadlines first.

    A careful, diplomatic suggestion.

  3. 3
    You should explain the situation honestly.

    A clear recommendation.

  4. 4
    If I were you, I would ask for more information.

    The speaker imagines being in the listener's position.

  5. 5
    You had better submit the form today.

    A strong warning: delay may cause a negative result.

Open a possibility

Questions can make advice less controlling

Have you considered + -ing...?

Have you considered asking for an extension?

Why don't you + base verb...?

Why don't you write down the advantages and disadvantages?

Word family

Advice is a noun; advise is a verb

advice /ədˈvaɪs/

She gave me some useful advice.

Uncountable noun: not an advice or advices.

advise /ədˈvaɪz/

She advised me to wait.

Verb: advise + person + to + verb.

Suggest and recommend

Use an -ing form or a complete that-clause

I suggest talking to the coordinator. / I suggest that you talk to the coordinator.

Not: I suggest you to talk to the coordinator.

Countable advice

Let me give you an advice.

Let me give you some advice.

Use some advice or a piece of advice.

Strong warning for a small option

You had better try a different notebook.

You could try a different notebook.

Use had better only when a real negative consequence makes the warning necessary.
Advice-scale modelHear the same basic suggestion become more direct and urgent.
09 Responding to adviceAccept, explore, limit, or decline advice without ending the conversation. +
Conversation skill

A response should show what you understood and what still affects the decision

A simple yes or no can stop the exchange. Natural responses acknowledge the advice, ask for detail, or explain a real limitation.

Accept

That makes sense. I will compare the deadlines.

Recognize

That is a good point. I had not considered the cost.

Ask for detail

What would you do first in my situation?

Partial acceptance

That might work, but I would need permission.

Respectful disagreement

I see what you mean, but I am not comfortable hiding the mistake.

Explain a constraint

I am not sure that would be possible because the deadline has passed.

Connected model

Advice becomes useful when the response adds information

Advisor: If I were you, I would speak to the department before rejecting the offer.

Student: That makes sense, but I would need a flexible schedule. Do you think I should explain that in my email?

Advisor: Yes. You could describe the conflict and ask whether another schedule is available.

Student: That is a good point. I had not considered asking for an alternative.

Response modelHear acceptance, a constraint, a follow-up question, and a respectful alternative.

11 Decision and goal phrasal verbsLearn the meaning, grammar, particle placement, and natural context. +
Correct classification

A phrasal or phrasal-prepositional verb contains a verb plus one or more particles

The particle helps create the meaning and may control where an object pronoun goes. Fixed collocations and idioms are presented separately in the next section.

Phrasal verb

verb + particle, with its own grammar

think the offer over
Fixed expression

words that regularly occur together

weigh the consequences
Idiom

a complete phrase with figurative meaning

be at a crossroads
separable phrasal verbconsiderar cuidadosamente

think something over

Meaning: consider something carefully before deciding.

Pronoun rule: think it over, not think over it.

  • I need to think the offer over.
  • Think it over before you reply.
inseparable phrasal verbdecidirse por / intentar conseguir

go for something

Meaning: choose, attempt, or actively pursue something.

Pattern: go for + noun.

  • She decided to go for the scholarship.
  • If the role fits your goals, go for it.
phrasal verbabandonar

give something up

Meaning: stop pursuing, using, or doing something.

Patterns: give it up; give up + -ing.

  • Do not give up your goal because of one obstacle.
  • He gave up trying to please everyone.
transitive / intransitiveresolver / resultar

work something out / work out

Meaning: find a solution, or develop successfully.

Context decides the meaning.

  • We worked out a practical compromise.
  • I hope the new schedule works out.
phrasal verbterminar / acabar

end up

Meaning: arrive at a final, sometimes unexpected result.

Patterns: end up + -ing; end up in/with.

  • She ended up accepting the position.
  • We could end up with fewer options.
phrasal-prepositional verbrecordar y evaluar

look back on something

Meaning: remember and evaluate a past period or decision.

Pattern: look back on + noun.

  • I look back on that year without regret.
  • When he looks back on the decision, he sees what he learned.

12 Fixed expressions and idiomsKeep literal collocations separate from figurative language. +
Two categories

Fixed expressions build natural combinations; idioms create a figurative picture

A fixed expression such as face a dilemma remains fairly literal. An idiom such as be at a crossroads uses a physical image to describe an important life decision.

Fixed expressions and collocations

Natural combinations for wishes, goals, and decisions

fixed expressionpedir un deseo

make a wish

Form or express a special desire.

  • Make a wish before you blow out the candles.
  • She made a wish for her family's health.
collocationperseguir un sueño

pursue a dream

Actively work toward an important aspiration.

  • He moved abroad to pursue his dream.
  • Pursuing a dream often requires a realistic plan.
collocationalcanzar una meta

achieve a goal

Successfully reach a planned result.

  • She achieved her goal after two years.
  • Small steps can help you achieve a larger goal.
collocationenfrentar un dilema

face a dilemma

Experience a difficult choice between competing options.

  • The committee faced an ethical dilemma.
  • I am facing a dilemma about the job offer.
collocationsopesar las consecuencias

weigh the consequences

Compare the possible results carefully.

  • Weigh the consequences before you decide.
  • She weighed the personal and financial consequences.
fixed expressionvivir con una decisión

live with a decision

Accept the continuing results of a choice.

  • I need to make a choice I can live with.
  • He learned to live with the decision.
Idioms

Figurative expressions for choices, dreams, and reflection

idiomestar en una encrucijada

be at a crossroads

Meaning: be at an important point where you must choose a direction.

  • I am at a crossroads in my career.
  • The organization is at a crossroads.
idiomun sueño hecho realidad

a dream come true

Meaning: something deeply desired that becomes real.

  • Winning the scholarship was a dream come true.
  • Opening the studio felt like a dream come true.
idiomtener dudas después de decidir

have second thoughts

Meaning: begin to doubt a decision you were ready to make.

  • I am having second thoughts about moving.
  • She accepted the offer but later had second thoughts.
idiomaprender de una experiencia difícil

learn the hard way

Meaning: learn through an unpleasant mistake or experience.

  • I learned the hard way to read every contract.
  • He learned the hard way that deadlines matter.
idiomsi dependiera de mí

if I had my way

Meaning: if I could control the decision completely.

  • If I had my way, every student would receive mentoring.
  • We would start next week if I had my way.

13 Sound practiceDecode I'd, connect would you, and hear the difference between advice and advise. +
Pronunciation support

Listen to the word after I'd to understand the contraction

I'd can mean I would or I had. The next verb reveals the grammar. Natural speech also connects and reduces frequent advice structures.

01

I'd + base verb

I'd means I would before a base verb.

I'd ACCEPT the offer.
02

I'd + past participle

I'd means I had before a past participle.

I wish I'd ASKED first.
03

would you /wʊdʒə/

In natural speech, final /d/ and initial /j/ often connect.

What would_you DO?
04

should have /ˈʃʊdəv/

The weak form may sound like “should of,” but the grammar is always should have.

I should've WAITed.
05

advice /s/ · advise /z/

The noun ends with an unvoiced /s/; the verb ends with a voiced /z/.

good adVICE · I adVISE you
06

Stress the contrast

Stress the imagined condition and the chosen consequence.

If I HAD more TIME, I'd STUDY abroad.

14 Complete integrated modelFollow one decision from hope and doubt to advice, regret, and a reasoned next step. +
Everything together

Valentina considers a scholarship in another city

This is teaching input, not an activity. Read or listen, then use the notes to understand every language choice.

Complete modelListen before or after reading the annotated decision.

Valentina hopes she gets a scholarship for a program in another city. She dreams of managing community projects, but she is at a crossroads. “I wish I were more certain,” she tells her counselor. “If I accepted the scholarship, I would have to leave my current job. What would you do if you were in my position?”

The counselor replies, “If I were you, I would think the offer over. You could weigh the financial consequences, and you might want to ask whether the program offers part-time work.” Valentina answers, “That makes sense. I regret turning down a similar opportunity two years ago. I wish I had asked for more information before I decided. I should have compared all the options.”

After the meeting, Valentina decides not to answer immediately. She will gather the facts first. “If the schedule worked out, I could accept the scholarship without giving up my job completely,” she thinks. She is still uncertain, but she now has a decision she can explain and live with.

wish, hope, or dreamconditionaladviceregretfixed expressionidiom

Why hope and dream?

The scholarship is still possible, so Valentina hopes she gets it. Managing community projects is a meaningful long-term aspiration, so she dreams of managing them.

Why wish I were?

Valentina is not certain now. The past form were marks the contrast with her present reality.

Why the second conditional?

She has not accepted the scholarship. If I accepted..., I would have to... explores an imagined decision and its consequence.

Why three advice forms?

If I were you gives a personal perspective. Could offers an option. Might want to makes the suggestion especially diplomatic.

Why three regret forms?

Regret turning down names the regretted action. Wish I had asked imagines the opposite past. Should have compared identifies the better past action.

Why classify the expressions?

Think over and give up are phrasal verbs. Weigh the consequences and live with a decision are fixed expressions. At a crossroads is an idiom.
