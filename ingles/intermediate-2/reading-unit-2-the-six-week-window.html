Vocabulary preview

Open the expressions you need

deferral

An official agreement to delay the start of something until a later date.

transition plan

A practical plan for transferring work and responsibility from one person to another.

hand something over

Transfer control or responsibility to another person.

step up

Accept more responsibility when it is needed.

count on someone

Trust someone to provide help or complete a responsibility.

meet a condition

Complete a requirement that must be satisfied before an agreement becomes final.

local coordinator

The person who organizes a project in its community or primary location.

Chapter 1 of 5

The first sentence

Salomé read the first sentence twice: “We may be able to defer the start of your scholarship for six weeks.” For a moment, she forgot to breathe. The offer had not disappeared, and the foundation had not demanded an immediate move. Because this option had arrived so late, the message also extended her reply deadline until noon the next day. However, the rest of the email made the situation more complicated.

The committee wanted a transition plan for the neighborhood library project by noon the next day. Salomé also had to attend a short online interview and name a local coordinator who could lead the project after she left. The deferral was possible, not automatic. If the committee accepted her plan, she could stay for the library launch and begin the program six weeks later.

“This changes the options,” Mr. Vega said, “but it does not make the decision for you. Read every condition before you answer.” Salomé wished she had more than thirteen hours, but she finally had the information she had failed to request two years earlier.

Reading pause: Which new possibility appears, and what must Salomé do to obtain it?

Chapter 2 of 5

An honest morning meeting

At seven thirty the next morning, Salomé met Lucía at the community library. Lucía had helped organize volunteers from the beginning, but Salomé had never told her about the scholarship application. When Salomé finished explaining, Lucía was quiet.

“I wish you had told me earlier,” she said. “I am happy for you, but the team should not learn about a major change at the last minute.” Salomé did not defend herself. She regretted trying to protect everyone from uncertainty because her silence had created a different kind of problem.

Then Lucía looked at the project calendar. “If you stayed until the launch, I could manage the weekly meetings afterward,” she said. “But I would need clear responsibilities, access to the budget and support from the other volunteers. Do not promise the foundation that everything is solved until you ask the team.”

Reading pause: Why is Lucía both disappointed and willing to help?

Chapter 3 of 5

A transition, not a rescue

Salomé and Lucía separated the project into four areas: volunteer schedules, the opening event, the reading program and the remaining design work. Lucía could step up as local coordinator, but she did not want to copy Salomé's way of doing everything. She proposed a small committee so that no single person would carry the entire project.

Salomé initially had second thoughts. She had imagined leading every detail until the final day. Handing the project over felt like giving up part of her dream. Lucía reminded her that the library belonged to the neighborhood, not to one organizer.

By nine o'clock, three volunteers had agreed to take specific responsibilities. The plan was not perfect: Salomé would have to finish the design documents in two weeks, train Lucía and accept that some decisions would be made without her. Still, the project would have a stronger team. Salomé began to understand that asking for help was not the same as abandoning a promise.

Reading pause: What must Salomé give up for the transition plan to work?

Chapter 4 of 5

The foundation interview

During the online interview, the committee asked why Salomé had not mentioned the project schedule in her original application. She answered honestly: she had hoped she could finish everything before leaving, and she had been afraid that a conflict would weaken her application.

Camila Torres, the program director, explained that Salomé's community work was one reason the committee had selected her. “We do not expect you to walk away from it,” she said. “We need evidence that the project can continue without depending entirely on you.”

Salomé presented the shared-responsibility plan and named Lucía as local coordinator. She also asked whether she could mentor the team online once a week. Camila said that occasional support was acceptable, but Salomé could not direct the project from another city. The six-week deferral would be approved if Lucía confirmed the arrangement and the committee received the final schedule before noon.

Reading pause: Why does the foundation limit Salomé's future role?

Chapter 5 of 5

The answer before noon

Lucía read the final plan carefully before signing it. “You can count on me,” she told Salomé, “but you must let us lead. If you try to control every decision from far away, neither the project nor your studies will receive your full attention.”

At eleven forty-two, Salomé accepted the scholarship and requested the six-week deferral. She would stay for the library launch, hand over leadership gradually and begin the urban design program after the transition. The compromise required more work, an uncomfortable conversation and less personal control. It did not remove every consequence.

Looking back, Salomé still wished she had involved the team sooner. Yet this time, regret did not close a door. It showed her what to do differently. Her dream could move forward because the community project no longer depended on one person—and because she had finally learned to ask before saying no.

Final evidence: Which sentence shows that the decision is responsible but not perfect?

Language detective

How the grammar changes the decision

“I wish you had told me earlier.”

What reality is implied?

Salomé did not tell Lucía earlier. Wish + past perfect imagines a different past.

She regretted trying to protect everyone from uncertainty.

Why regret + -ing?

The structure names a past action that now causes disappointment.

“If you stayed until the launch, I could manage the weekly meetings.”

What does the conditional express?

Lucía imagines one possible arrangement and its consequence before the plan is confirmed.

“You should not promise that everything is solved until you ask the team.”

How strong is the advice?

Should not gives clear advice while leaving the final responsibility with Salomé.

Lucía could step up, and Salomé would hand the project over.

Which phrasal verbs appear?

Step up means accept more responsibility. Hand something over means transfer control or responsibility.

Salomé had second thoughts, but she learned she could count on the team.

Idiom or phrasal-prepositional verb?

Have second thoughts is an idiom for reconsidering. Count on is a phrasal-prepositional verb meaning trust or depend on.
