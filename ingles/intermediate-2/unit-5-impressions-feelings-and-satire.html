01Start with what you can seeAn observation is something visible. A deduction is your explanation of it.+
Understand the meaning

Start with what you can see

An observation is something visible. A deduction is your explanation of it.

One scene, several explanations

Observation: She is checking the time.

Another possibility: She could be worried about being late. The picture does not tell us who she is waiting for.

Add information before you decide

Observation: He is yawning.

Extra information: He has worked all night.

The extra information supports a strong deduction.

Keep another explanation open

Observation: She is holding her notes.

A gesture can have more than one explanation.

  1. 1 · ObserveSay what you can actually see or hear.
  2. 2 · InterpretChoose a level of certainty.
  3. 3 · SupportGive evidence and consider another explanation.
A picture is a clue, not proof

Do not decide someone’s job, personality or background from their clothes or face alone. Ask for information when you can.

02Must, might and can’tChoose a modal to show how strongly the evidence supports your idea.+
Understand the meaning

Must, might and can’t

Choose a modal to show how strongly the evidence supports your idea.

Strong negative deductioncan’t beI am almost sure this is not true. Can’t means cannot.
Possible explanationmight · may · couldPerhaps it is true. I am not sure; another explanation is possible.
Strong positive deductionmust beI am almost sure this is true because of the clues. This is a conclusion, not an order.
What does be mean here?

Be is the base form of am, is and are. After a modal, use be: She is tired → She might be tired. Might be means perhaps she is; must be means I am almost sure she is; can’t be means I am almost sure she is not.

These are not exact percentages

These words express your judgement. Even a strong deduction can turn out to be wrong when new information appears.

MeaningExampleWhy this word?
must be · strong deductionI am almost sure someone is inside. The lights and voices give strong evidence.
might be · possibilityPerhaps someone is inside, but I am not sure. The lights could be on even when nobody is there.
can’t be · strong negative deductionI am almost sure that person is not Leo. His location is evidence against the idea.
Other possibilitiesMay, might and could all introduce possibilities here.

Might, may and could: what is the difference?

WordHow it sounds hereExample
mightA tentative guess: it is possible, but I am not sure.
mayAlso means perhaps. It can sound a little more formal than might.
couldPresents a possible explanation or alternative. It also works in a question about a possibility now.
Often, all three work

She might / may / could be tired all mean that tiredness is possible. There is no fixed certainty order. Here, may is not permission and could is not past ability. For a question about a possibility, Could she be…? is a natural choice.

Build it in three useful ways

SubjectShe+Modalmight+Base verbknow+More informationthe answer.

A state or description

Meaning: Perhaps she is tired. We are describing how she feels, not an action.

modal + be + adjective
might = possible · be = base form of is · tired = a description.

She is tired → She might be tired.
Tired is an adjective, so it needs be here. Do not say “She might tired” or “She might is tired.”

An action happening now

Meaning: Perhaps she is waiting for a friend right now. Other explanations are possible.

modal + be + verb-ing
might = possible · be waiting = an action in progress.

She is waiting → She might be waiting.
Use be before waiting. Do not say “She might waiting.”

Another verb

Meaning: Perhaps she knows the answer. We are not sure.

modal + base verb
might = possible · know = the main verb in its base form.

She knows → She might know.
Know is already a verb, so do not add be, to or -s: not “might be know,” “might to know” or “might knows.”

The same forms work with must and can’t

Change the modal to change certainty, while keeping the verb form: must be tired / can’t be tired; must be waiting / can’t be waiting; must know / can’t know. Choose the modal only when the clues support that meaning.

One word, a different job: must

Deduction: what I conclude

I have evidence. I strongly believe that he is tired.

Obligation: what a rule requires

This is a requirement. It does not describe how certain I feel.

Can’t, mustn’t and might not are different

ExpressionExampleMeaning
can’t + verbStrong negative deduction in this context.
mustn’t + verbA rule forbids entry. This is prohibition.
might not + verbPerhaps she is not ready. We are not sure.
don’t have to + verbIt is not necessary. It is allowed, but optional.
Context changes the meaning

“I can’t swim” describes ability. “You can’t park here” can express a rule. “That can’t be true” expresses a deduction. Read the complete situation.

Ask about a possibility

Put the modal before the subject. You can answer: “Yes, she might be.” Here, could describes a present possibility; it is not automatically about the past.

Common errors, explained

Avoid She must to be tired.

Use

Use the base verb after a modal, without to.

Avoid He might is nervous.

Use

The base form of is is be.

Avoid She must knows him.

Use

The modal does not take -s, and the next verb stays in its base form.

03Looks, seems and looks likeDescribe an impression without presenting it as a confirmed fact.+
Understand the meaning

Looks, seems and looks like

Describe an impression without presenting it as a confirmed fact.

What her appearance suggests

You are using visible clues, such as her expression or posture.

What the situation suggests

Your impression may come from her words, actions or the situation, not only her face.

A person can seem interesting

This is your impression of the guide. It does not describe the guide’s own feeling.

PatternExampleHow it works
look + adjectiveLooks describes her appearance. Confident is an adjective.
seem + adjectiveSeems gives a general impression.
look like + noun phraseA volunteer is a noun phrase. The appearance is only a clue.
look like + complete ideaHe needs help has its own subject and verb.
seem + to + base verbUse to when another verb follows seems.
be + adjectiveYou present the feeling as a fact, perhaps because she told you.

Follow the word after look

SubjectShe+Appearance verblooks+Adjectivetired.
SubjectShe+Appearance verblooks+Linklike+Noun phrasea tired student.
Compare look like and look at

“Look like” describes a resemblance or impression. “Look at the picture” asks someone to direct their eyes towards something.

Questions, negatives and subject agreement

One person / several people

Use looks/seems with he, she or it. Use look/seem with I, you, we or they.

Ask

Does already marks the subject: use seem, not seems.

Make it negative

This describes the appearance. It does not prove how she feels inside.

Common errors, explained

Avoid She looks like tired.

Use

Use an adjective directly after looks.

Avoid He seems know everyone.

Use

Use seems to before another base verb.

Avoid Does she looks nervous?

Use

After does, use the base form look.

04Bored or boring? The feeling and its causeThe ending changes the meaning. Both endings can describe people.+
Understand the meaning

Bored or boring? The feeling and its cause

The ending changes the meaning. Both endings can describe people.

-ed: how someone feels

Use these -ed adjectives for the person who experiences the feeling. Ask: How does this person feel?

I am not interested in what is happening. This describes my feeling.

-ing: what causes the feeling

Use these -ing adjectives for the person, thing or situation that produces the feeling. Ask: What is it like? How does it make someone feel?

The talk makes me feel bored. This describes the cause.

A person can have either ending

I am bored means I feel little interest. I am boring means I make other people feel bored. Both are possible English, but they say different things. The rule is about feeling versus cause, not people versus things.

Compare each feeling with its cause

/

Feeling · bored

I feel little interest. I want something more interesting to happen.

Cause · boring

The talk does not hold my attention. It makes me feel bored.

/

Feeling · interested

The visitors want to listen, learn or know more.

Cause · interesting

The guide holds their attention and makes them want to learn more.

/

Feeling · excited

The students feel happy and full of energy about the project.

Cause · exciting

The project creates that happy, energetic feeling.

/

Feeling · confused

I do not understand clearly. I am not sure what to do.

Cause · confusing

The instructions are difficult to understand. They cause the confusion.

/

Feeling · surprised

We did not expect the result. This describes our reaction.

Cause · surprising

The result was unexpected. It caused our surprise.

/

Feeling · embarrassed

He feels uncomfortable or shy because of the mistake, especially in front of others.

Cause · embarrassing

The mistake creates that uncomfortable feeling.

He looks bored

The person feels it. His expression suggests that he is not interested.

The guide is interesting

A person can cause the feeling. The visitors want to hear more.

  1. CauseThe instructions are confusing.
  2. EffectI do not understand them.
  3. FeelingI am confused.

Combine the feeling and the reason

FeelingI am confused+Reason linkbecause+Causethe instructions are confusing.
Why do we use am, is or are?

In these examples, bored and boring are adjectives: they describe a feeling or a quality. Use be + adjective: I am bored; the talk is boring. You can also use feel or look to describe a feeling: I feel bored; he looks bored.

The ending does not tell you the time

I am bored describes a feeling now; I was bored describes it in the past. The -ed ending does not make these sentences past tense. In The talk is boring, boring is a description; in She is waiting, waiting is an action in progress.

Listen to the endings

One syllable, two syllables

Bored ends with /d/. Boring has two syllables. Do not add a new syllable to bored.

The final sound

Keep the /d/ in confused. In confusing, the final sound is /ŋ/, as in sing.

Not every -ed sounds the same

Excited ends with /ɪd/, surprised with /d/, and embarrassed with /t/. Hear the model rather than saying every ending the same way.

Two useful limits

Not all emotion adjectives have an -ed/-ing pair: happy and sad do not. Also, an -ing word after be can describe an action: “She is waiting.” In “The talk is boring,” boring describes the talk.

Avoid I am boring. (when I mean that I feel no interest)

Use

I am boring means other people find me uninteresting. Both sentences are grammatical, but their meanings differ.

Avoid The instructions are confused. (when I mean difficult to follow)

Use

Use confusing for the cause. Use confused for the person who does not understand.

05I think, I guess and perhapsTell the listener that you are giving an opinion or a possible explanation.+
Understand the meaning

I think, I guess and perhaps

Tell the listener that you are giving an opinion or a possible explanation.

ExpressionExampleUse
I think + complete ideaMeaning: This is what I believe. Here, I believe she is worried, but I am presenting my opinion rather than a confirmed fact. Use it to share an impression and add a reason when you can.
I guess + complete ideaMeaning: This is my best guess. I do not have enough information to be sure. Here, waiting for a friend is one explanation I can imagine. I guess is common in informal conversation.
Perhaps / Maybe + complete ideaMeaning: It is possible that she needs more time. Perhaps and maybe both mean that something is possible. Maybe is common in conversation; perhaps can sound a little more formal.
I don’t think + complete ideaMeaning: In this everyday use, I believe he is probably not angry. Put don’t before think: I don’t think + he is angry. This is a negative opinion, not proof of how he feels.
Choose the expression for your message

I think introduces a belief or opinion. I guess presents a tentative, informal answer. Perhaps / Maybe presents a possibility. These meanings overlap: they are not fixed percentages of certainty. Your reason and tone also matter.

What is a complete idea?

Opinion starterI think+Subjectshe+Verbis+Descriptionworried.

She is worried has its own subject and verb. Put I think or I guess before a complete idea. Perhaps and maybe can also introduce one: Perhaps + she needs more time. Do not leave out she or the verb.

Maybe and may be: similar sound, different structure

Maybe: one word

Meaning: Perhaps she is tired.

Maybe + subject + verb + description
Maybe introduces the whole idea. She is tired still needs is.

Maybe is one word meaning perhaps; it is not the verb.

May be: two words

Meaning: It is possible that she is tired.

Subject + may + be + description
May expresses possibility. Be is the base form of is after the modal.

May be is two words: a modal and a verb. Do not write “She maybe tired.”

Hear a respectful disagreement

Sam

Alex

  1. 1 · Give an impressionAlex thinks she is upset and gives a clue: she is quiet.
  2. 2 · Offer another possibilitySam accepts that idea as possible, then suggests tiredness. He does not claim Alex is wrong.
  3. 3 · Check the ideaAlex accepts that Sam may be right and suggests asking her. Asking gives better information than guessing.
An opinion is not evidence

“I think” does not make a claim true. Add what you saw, heard or checked. Avoid assuming that a quiet person is unfriendly.

Avoid I think is tired.

Use

The idea after I think needs its own subject.

Avoid She maybe tired.

Use

After the subject, use the modal may and the verb be. Alternatively: Maybe she is tired.

06Because, so, but, although and thoughConnect the same ideas in different ways: reason, result or contrast.+
Understand the meaning

Because, so, but, although and though

Connect the same ideas in different ways: reason, result or contrast.

Reason and result

Known information: He worked all night.

Contrast

Feeling nervous does not always stop someone from speaking clearly.

LinkWhat follows itExample
becauseA reason with a subject and verb.
because ofA noun phrase.
soA result or conclusion.
butAn idea that contrasts with the first.
althoughAn idea that contrasts with the main message.
though at the endAn informal contrast with the previous idea.

Two positions for although

When the although part comes first, separate it with a comma. When it comes after the main message, a comma is usually unnecessary in these simple examples.

  1. Reason → conclusionHe worked all night → so he must be tired.
  2. Conclusion → reasonHe must be tired → because he worked all night.
  3. Unexpected contrastAlthough she is nervous → she speaks clearly.

Avoid Although she is nervous, but she speaks clearly.

Use

Choose although or but for this connection. Do not use both together.

Avoid Because he worked all night, so he is tired.

Use

Use because for the reason, or so for the result. One connector is enough here.

Avoid Because of she is tired, she is resting.

Use

Use because before a subject and verb. Use because of before a noun phrase.

Sound: though and thought

Though ends with the vowel /oʊ/. Thought ends with /t/. Although and though share the voiced /ð/ sound of this. Listen to the complete models.

07Feelings in contextLearn the word with a situation and a useful phrase.+
Understand the meaning

Feelings in context

Learn the word with a situation and a useful phrase.

The same scene can suggest different feelings. These are example contexts, not certain descriptions of the people in the photographs.

Worried or tense before something important.

Glad that a worry or difficulty is over.

Thinking that something bad might happen.

Feeling sure about your ability.

Feeling little interest in what is happening.

Wanting to know or learn more.

Needing rest.

Pleased about something you or another person achieved.

Uncomfortable because of a social mistake or unwanted attention.

Upset because something stops you from doing what you want.

Sad because something was not as good as you hoped.

Feeling thankful for help or kindness.

Close meanings, different focus

Worried or nervous?

Worried: you think about a possible problem.

Nervous: you feel tense, often before an event. You can feel both.

Frustrated or disappointed?

Frustrated: an obstacle stops your progress.

Disappointed: a result does not meet your hopes.

A mood or a personality?

She feels nervous today: a current feeling.

She is usually confident: a more general description. One moment does not tell you everything about a person.

08Phrasal verbs and idiomsUse expressions to describe support, relationships and reactions.+
Understand the meaning

Phrasal verbs and idioms

Use expressions to describe support, relationships and reactions.

What is a phrasal verb?

A verb with a small word such as up, down or out. Together, they have a particular meaning. Some expressions also need a word such as with, for or to.

Cheer up means feel happier or help someone feel happier.

What is an idiom?

An expression whose meaning is different from the literal meaning of its words.

Break the ice means make a first meeting feel more comfortable. There is no real ice.

The Spanish meanings below are approximate equivalents for these situations, not word-for-word translations.

Phrasal verb

Feel happier, or help someone feel happier.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Animarse / animar a alguien

With a pronoun: cheer her up, not cheer up her.

Phrasal verb

Become less tense or upset.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Calmarse

A direct order like Calm down! can sound impatient. Try a supportive suggestion.

Phrasal verb

Talk more openly about your feelings.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Abrirse y hablar de los sentimientos

Use to before the person you talk to. Do not pressure someone to share.

Phrasal verb

Feel accepted as part of a group.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Encajar / sentirse integrado

Use with to name the group.

Phrasal verb

Have a friendly relationship with someone.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Llevarse bien con

Keep with before the person. Get on with is also common.

Phrasal verb

Contact someone to offer or ask for help.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Contactar para ofrecer o pedir ayuda

Keep to before the person or organization.

Phrasal verb

Defend or support someone or something.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Defender / apoyar

Keep the complete expression together.

Phrasal verb

Admire and respect someone.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Admirar / respetar

Look like describes resemblance. Look up to expresses admiration.

Idiom

Make a first meeting feel more comfortable.

Approximate meaning in Spanish
Romper el hielo

Informal; no real ice is involved.

Idiom

Feel a little ill.

Approximate meaning in Spanish

Be extremely happy.

Approximate meaning in Spanish

Informal; often a reaction to very good news.

Idiom

Be very kind and generous.

Approximate meaning in Spanish

A warm compliment supported by the person’s actions.

Use the complete expression

The small words matter: open up to someone, stand up for someone and look up to someone. Listen to the whole phrase as one group.

09Use the grammar: people, community and mediaRead complete models that separate facts, impressions and interpretations.+
Understand the meaning

Use the grammar: people, community and media

Read complete models that separate facts, impressions and interpretations.

Making a good impression

Describe a helpful action

Listen without interrupting. Ask a friendly question. Offer help rather than assuming someone needs it.

Describe a contribution

Use a concrete action to explain a positive opinion.

Keep the opinion fair

Someone can be quiet and friendly at the same time.

Community model · a fictional example

Facts in the story: she organizes a group and welcomes members. Opinions: I think she is considerate; her work makes a difference. Deduction: she must be busy.

News and satire: check the source before you conclude

TypeWhat it doesWhat to look for
Fact-based reportReports information supported by a source.Who said it? When? What evidence is available?
OpinionGives a person’s judgement.Reasons, values and language such as I think.
SatireUses humor, exaggeration or irony to comment on something.A satirical context, an impossible exaggeration or a clear humorous target.
False claimPresents inaccurate information as true.Check evidence. False information is not automatically satire.

Fictional teaching example · plain report

The community library has extended its evening opening hours.

Fictional teaching example · SATIRE

Library books demand overtime pay after the building stays open late.

The second example gives books a human action to create humor. It comments on the longer hours. In real news, check the source and context too: an unusual headline alone is not proof of satire.
