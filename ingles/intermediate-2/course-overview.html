Learn first

Relative clauses: identify people clearly

When you talk about relationships, the listener often needs to know exactly which person you mean. Relative clauses help you add that information without starting a second sentence.

People

Use who for people

Form: person + who + action or description.

Sofia is the neighbor who helped me when I moved in.

I have a friend who always checks in when I disappear.

People or things

Use that in common speech

Form: person / thing + that + useful detail.

He's the classmate that invited me to the study group.

It's an app that helps people meet safely.

Too repetitive

My neighbor is Daniel. Daniel lives upstairs. Daniel helped me with my Wi-Fi.

More natural

Daniel is the neighbor who lives upstairs and helped me with my Wi-Fi.

Grammar guide

How to build the sentence

1. Name or identify the person.

Maria is my neighbor.

2. Add who or that.

Maria is my neighbor who...

3. Add the detail that makes the person clear.

Maria is my neighbor who takes care of my plants when I travel.

Use it to avoid repetition

Instead of two short sentences, combine the information when the second sentence explains the person.

I met a woman. She runs the building chat. → I met the woman who runs the building chat.

Do not repeat the pronoun

After who or that, do not add another subject if the relative pronoun is already the subject.

Not: She's the friend who she calls me every Sunday.

Yes: She's the friend who calls me every Sunday.

Relationship language

Words for the people in your social circle

The word you choose changes the relationship. A neighbor is not always a friend, and a new contact is not always someone you know well.

neighbora person who lives near you

She's the neighbor who feeds my cat when I travel.

close friendsomeone you trust a lot

He's a close friend who knows my family well.

old friendsomeone from your past

She's an old friend that I got back in touch with last year.

classmatesomeone in your class

He's the classmate who helped me prepare for the exam.

coworkersomeone you work with

She's a coworker who is kind of quiet but very helpful.

new contactsomeone you recently met

He's a new contact that I met at a language event.

Speak with tact

Soften personality and behavior descriptions

English often uses softeners when a description could sound too direct. kind of, a bit and a little bit make your sentence more careful and natural.

Too blunt More tactful Why it works
He's rude. He can be a bit direct. It describes the behavior without attacking the person.
She's strange. She's kind of quiet at first. It gives context and avoids a negative label.
They are annoying. They can be a little bit intense in group chats. It names the situation instead of judging everything about them.
Useful formula:

Person + can be + softener + adjective + situation.

My neighbor can be a bit noisy on weekends, but she's very friendly.

Phrasal verbs

The relationship timeline

These expressions are easier to remember as a story: you meet someone, discover common interests, build contact and maybe reconnect later.

  1. get to knowSpend time learning about someone.I got to know Laura during an English project.
  2. hit it offLike each other quickly.We hit it off because we both love hiking.
  3. have a lot in commonShare interests, values or experiences.We have a lot in common, especially music and travel.
  4. get on wellHave a good relationship.I get on well with my neighbors.
  5. keep in touchContinue communicating.We keep in touch through voice messages.
  6. catch upTalk after some time and share news.Let's catch up after class.
  7. get back in touchContact someone again after a long time.I got back in touch with an old school friend.
