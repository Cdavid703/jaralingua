Reading

The Saturday Table: New Faces, Old Connections

5 chapters
1. Quiet week 2. Invitation 3. Familiar face 4. Impressions 5. Contact

Chapter 1 of 5

A quiet first week

Nora Salcedo moved into apartment 4B on a Monday afternoon. She had accepted a new job at a small design studio, and the new apartment was closer to work. The location was convenient, but her first week felt unusually quiet. She passed people in the elevator, smiled politely and returned to a home that was still full of boxes.

The only person Nora spoke to more than once was Mr. Okafor, the neighbor who lived across the hall. He always greeted her by name and asked whether she needed help. He seemed a little formal because he checked every detail before he spoke, but he was never unkind. On Friday evening, he knocked on her door with an invitation.

Reading pause: What two details show that Nora has not formed a social circle yet?

Chapter 2 of 5

The invitation

“We have the Saturday Table once a month,” Mr. Okafor explained. “It is a dinner that helps new residents meet their neighbors. You do not need to bring anything, but people usually share a simple dish.”

Nora almost said no. Large groups could make her feel a bit uncomfortable, especially when she knew nobody. However, she also understood that another quiet weekend would not help her meet anyone. On Saturday, she prepared a fruit salad and went down to the courtyard. Several small tables had been joined together under warm lights. Mr. Okafor, who organized the dinner, introduced her to the other residents without making her feel like the center of attention.

Reading pause: Why does Nora accept an invitation that makes her nervous?

Chapter 3 of 5

A familiar face

The first person Nora sat beside was Gabriel Costa, a photographer who had recently returned to the city. They discovered that they both enjoyed walking through old neighborhoods and photographing public spaces. The conversation felt easy, and they hit it off almost immediately. Gabriel became the new contact that shared Nora’s interest in city stories. Before dinner ended, they exchanged contact details and discussed a weekend photography walk.

Then Nora heard a familiar laugh at the other end of the table. The woman looked different with shorter hair, but Nora recognized her. It was Iris Chen, the classmate who had helped Nora prepare an important presentation six years earlier. They had lost contact after Iris moved to another city. For a few seconds, both women looked surprised. Then Iris stood up, hugged Nora and said, “I cannot believe we live in the same building.”

Reading pause: How are Gabriel and Iris different kinds of connections?

Chapter 4 of 5

First impressions

During dinner, Nora noticed that Mr. Okafor checked the table, moved chairs and reminded people where to place empty dishes. At first, his careful manner seemed kind of serious. Gabriel quietly explained that Mr. Okafor was the neighbor who contacted the building manager when something needed repair. He also visited residents who were sick and carried groceries for an older couple upstairs.

Nora realized that her first impression had been incomplete. Mr. Okafor was not controlling or unfriendly; he was responsible. His attention to detail was the reason the dinner felt welcoming and organized. Later, when a glass of water spilled, he did not complain. He brought a towel, made a quick joke and continued the conversation.

Reading pause: Which evidence changes Nora’s opinion of Mr. Okafor?

Chapter 5 of 5

Keeping in touch

By the end of the evening, Nora’s social circle had not changed because of one dramatic moment. It had changed through several small conversations. She now knew a neighbor who made the building feel safer, an old classmate she wanted to get back in touch with, and a new contact that she got on well with.

Iris invited Nora for coffee the following week. Gabriel added her to the photography walk, and Mr. Okafor included her in the building’s community group. As Nora carried the empty fruit bowl upstairs, the hallway did not feel as silent as it had on Monday. The people around her were no longer strangers with apartment numbers. They were people who now had names, stories and possible places in her life.

For Nora, the evening proved that belonging could begin with one invitation and the courage to accept it.

Reading pause: Which small actions will help the four characters keep in touch?

Language detective

What does each relative clause do?

Mr. Okafor is the neighbor who lived across the hall.

Why who?

The clause identifies a person. Who is the safest and most natural choice.

It is a dinner that helps new residents meet.

Why that?

The noun is a thing or event, so that works naturally in an identifying clause.

Mr. Okafor, who organized the dinner, introduced Nora.

Why commas and who?

The name already identifies him. The clause adds extra information, so it uses commas and who, not that.

Gabriel became the new contact that shared Nora’s interest.

Can a person use that?

Yes, in an identifying clause without commas. Who would also be correct and is usually the safest choice.

2. How did Nora know Iris before the dinner?

3. Why do Nora and Gabriel hit it off?

5. What does “several small conversations” suggest?

Pair talk

Reconstruct the relationships aloud

Student A asks about one person. Student B answers with a complete relative clause and evidence.

Student A: Who is Mr. Okafor?

Student B: He is the neighbor who invited Nora to the Saturday Table.

Student A: Who did Nora get back in touch with?

Student B: She got back in touch with Iris, an old classmate who had helped her with a presentation.

Teacher: What evidence changes Nora’s first impression?

Student B: Mr. Okafor helps residents and solves problems calmly, so Nora realizes that he is responsible.

The dinner gives Nora three new possibilities. Mr. Okafor is the neighbor who invites her and who helps the building. Iris is an old classmate that Nora had lost contact with, so the dinner helps them get back in touch. Gabriel is a new contact who shares Nora’s interest in photography. They hit it off and exchange contact details. These conversations change Nora’s first impression of the building and give her practical ways to keep in touch. Her hallway feels less anonymous because every connection now has a story.
