01 Relative clauses for peopleChoose who or that by clause type, not by the action. +
The key answer

Who and that do not describe different kinds of actions.

Sofia is the neighbor who helped me and He is the classmate that invited me have the same basic grammar: a person + an identifying relative clause. The verbs helped and invited do not determine the pronoun. In a defining clause, both choices can be correct. Who is the clearest and safest choice for a person; that is a common alternative in neutral or casual speech.

Meaning

What is a relative clause?

It is a group of words that follows a noun and adds information about that person. It lets you combine ideas without repeating the person.

I met a neighbor. She organizes the building chat.
I met the neighbor who organizes the building chat.

Core form

Person + who/that + information

Use a person noun first, then the relative pronoun, then the action or description that identifies the person.

person + who/that + verb + complement

She is the friend who checks in every week.

Decision 1

Defining relative clauses: the information identifies the person

A defining clause answers Which person? The sentence needs this information because the noun is not specific enough by itself. Do not use commas.

Default for people

Use who

Who clearly signals a person and works in casual, academic, and professional English.

The neighbor who lives upstairs is trustworthy.

Possible in defining clauses

Use that

That is also grammatical when the clause is essential and has no commas. It is frequent in everyday conversation.

The classmate that sits near me is very helpful.

Student shortcut: If the noun is a person and you are unsure, use who. You will be correct in the widest range of situations.
Decision 2

Non-defining relative clauses: the information is extra

A non-defining clause adds a comment about a person who is already clearly identified by a name, a possessive, or the context. Put a comma before and after the clause. Use who, never that.

Defining · no commas

My neighbor who works nights is usually asleep in the morning.

The clause identifies which neighbor. The speaker may have several neighbors.

Non-defining · commas

Mr. Okafor, who works nights, is usually asleep in the morning.

Mr. Okafor is already identified. His schedule is extra information.

Not: Iris, that was in my evening class, is an old friend.

Correct: Iris, who was in my evening class, is an old friend.

Sentence role

Is the relative pronoun the subject or the object?

Subject: do not omit it

The relative pronoun performs the action. It must stay in the sentence.

person + who/that + verb

The woman who called me is my neighbor.

Not: The woman called me is my neighbor.

Object: omission is possible

Another subject performs the action. In a defining clause, who or that may be omitted.

person + (who/that) + subject + verb

The woman (who/that) I called is my neighbor.

In formal English, whom is possible, but everyday speech usually uses who or no pronoun.

Accuracy check

Common mistakes and why they happen

Repeated subject

She is the friend who she calls me every Sunday.

She is the friend who calls me every Sunday.

Who is already the subject of calls.

That after a comma

Lina, that lives next door, is a nurse.

Lina, who lives next door, is a nurse.

Non-defining clauses for people require who.

Who for a thing

It is an app who connects people.

It is an app that connects people.

Who refers to people, not things.

Unnecessary comma

The neighbor, who has the spare key, is away.

The neighbor who has the spare key is away.

If the clause identifies which neighbor, do not separate it with commas.

02 People in your social circleRelationship words plus neighborhood character and behavior vocabulary. +
Meaning first

What are neighbors, and who are your neighbors?

A neighbor is a person who lives near you. A neighbor may become a friend, but the words are not synonyms. A friend is someone with whom you have a personal relationship; a close friend is someone you know and trust deeply.

neighborvecino/a

a person who lives near you

Mr. Okafor is the neighbor who lives across the hall.
close friendamigo/a cercano/a

someone you trust and know very well

Nadia is a close friend who knows my family.
old friendamigo/a de hace tiempo

a friend you have known for a long time or knew in the past

Iris is an old friend that I recently contacted again.
classmatecompañero/a de clase

a person who studies in the same class

Owen is the classmate who shares his notes with me.
coworkercompañero/a de trabajo

a person who works with you

Leila is a coworker who gives honest advice.
new contactcontacto nuevo

a person you met recently and may communicate with again

Gabriel is a new contact that I met at dinner.
person I met onlinepersona que conocí en línea

someone first known through an online space

Jon is a friend who I first met online.
person I lost contact withpersona con quien perdí contacto

someone you stopped communicating with

Ari is a school friend that I lost contact with.
acquaintanceconocido/a

someone you know, but not very well

Mara is an acquaintance who works in my building.
partnerpareja

a person in a committed romantic relationship

Sam is her partner, who works in public health.
Neighborhood language

Character, boundaries, and community life

favor

Meaning: a helpful act.

Pattern: do someone a favor / ask for a favor.

The neighbor who did me a favor is very kind.

trustworthy

Meaning: honest and reliable enough to trust.

Use: people, services, or sources.

I gave the spare key to a trustworthy neighbor.

nosy / nosey

Meaning: too interested in other people's private lives.

Nuance: usually negative; nosy is the more common spelling.

A nosy neighbor asks questions that feel too personal.

noisy

Meaning: making a lot of sound.

Do not confuse: nosy asks about private matters; noisy makes noise.

The apartment upstairs can be noisy at night.

neighborhood

Meaning: the local area where people live.

Note: A neighbor is a person; a neighborhood is a place.

Our neighborhood has a strong community group.

conflict

Meaning: a serious disagreement or problem between people.

Pattern: have / avoid / resolve a conflict.

They resolved the conflict without sharing gossip.

gossip

Meaning: unverified personal information shared about other people.

Grammar: a noun or a verb; normally uncountable as a noun.

Trustworthy friends do not spread gossip.

03 Describe people with tactUse kind of, a bit, and a little bit without sounding judgmental. +
Communicative purpose

Soften a description when a direct label may sound too strong

Softeners do not make an unkind comment automatically polite. They reduce the force of an adjective. The most respectful pattern describes observable behavior in a situation, not the person's complete identity.

Informal

kind of + adjective

Common in conversation. It signals that the description is approximate.

She is kind of quiet when she meets new people.

Often mildly negative

a bit + adjective

Means “slightly.” It often softens criticism or an uncomfortable quality.

He can be a bit direct in group chats.

Gentle and conversational

a little bit + adjective

Similar to a bit, but its longer form can sound more careful.

The first date was a little bit awkward at first.

Natural pattern

Person + can be + softener + adjective + situation

person + can be + kind of / a bit / a little bit + adjective + context

Too absolute

My neighbor is rude.

Behavior with context

My neighbor can be a bit direct when the building is noisy.

Too judgmental

My coworker is strange.

Balanced description

My coworker is kind of quiet at first, but she is very friendly.

Form check

Place the softener before the adjective, not before the noun

Not: He is a bit noisy neighbor.

He is a bit noisy.

He is a slightly noisy neighbor.

Not: She kind quiet.

She is kind of quiet.

Kind of is a two-word softener here.

Not: They are a little bit people.

They are a little bit reserved.

The softener modifies an adjective.

Unbalanced: He is a bit annoying.

More useful: He can be a bit intense in meetings, but he listens to feedback.

Add a situation and, when honest, a balancing quality.

04 Dating and meeting peopleRecognize common ways adults meet and the language used before and during dates. +
Unit context

People meet in many ways, and each expression describes a different stage

Meeting someone does not automatically mean dating them. English distinguishes the first meeting, an invitation, a date, a casual relationship, and a committed partnership.

Through people

through friends or family

Someone introduces two people who may have shared interests.

Shared context

at work, in class, or at an event

People get to know each other while doing something together.

Online

dating site or dating app

A digital service helps people find and contact possible dates.

Organized event

speed dating

Participants have several very short conversations with possible dates.

Introduced date

blind date

Two people meet for a date without knowing each other well beforehand.

Relationship sequence

From a first meeting to a relationship

  1. 1
    meet someone

    have a first encounter: I met Jordan at a community event.

  2. 2
    get to know someone

    learn about the person over time: We got to know each other during the project.

  3. 3
    ask someone out

    invite the person on a romantic date: Jordan asked Alex out for coffee.

  4. 4
    go on a date

    spend planned romantic time together: They went on a date on Saturday.

  5. 5
    see / date someone

    have an ongoing romantic connection: They are seeing each other.

  6. 6
    be in a relationship / be partners

    have a more established commitment: They have been partners for two years.

Adult vocabulary

Terms that require context and care

chivalry

Traditional polite or protective behavior, especially in dating. Some people appreciate it; others prefer equal, explicitly discussed expectations. It is culturally dependent.

They discussed whether opening doors is chivalry or simply courtesy.

fling

A short, casual romantic or sexual relationship that is not expected to become serious. It is informal and can sound judgmental if used about someone else without care.

They described it as a summer fling, not a long-term relationship.

one-night stand

One sexual encounter with no expected continuing relationship. This is explicit adult vocabulary; use it only when the context genuinely requires the term.

The article distinguishes a one-night stand from an ongoing casual relationship.

Respectful language: Describe the relationship people say they have. Do not infer intimacy or commitment only because two people went on a date.
Integrated description

Combine the unit language

Rafael is the coworker who organized our fundraiser. He is kind of quiet at first, but he is very thoughtful. He met Camila through friends and eventually asked her out for coffee.

The relative clause identifies Rafael, the softener makes the personality description tactful, and the dating expression explains the next step.

05 Relationship phrasal verbsOnly real verb + particle/preposition combinations. Idioms and fixed expressions are separated below. +
Classification: phrasal verbs only

A phrasal verb changes or completes the meaning of the main verb

A phrasal verb normally combines a main verb with a particle or required preposition. In this unit, students should not call every multiword expression a phrasal verb. Catch up, hit it off, ask someone out, and meet up are phrasal verbs because the verb works together with the particle. Get on well with is a phrasal-prepositional verb because the full pattern needs both on and with.

Phrasal verb

Verb + particle/preposition. The combination acts like one vocabulary item.

Example: ask someone out = invitar a alguien a salir.
Fixed expression

A common phrase with a stable pattern. It is useful, but it is not necessarily a phrasal verb.

Example: have a lot in common = tener mucho en común.
Idiom

A figurative expression. The full meaning is not literal word by word.

Example: a shoulder to cry on = alguien que te apoya emocionalmente.
phrasal verbponerse al día

catch up (with someone)

Meaning: talk after time apart and exchange recent news.

Use it when: you already know the person but have not spoken recently.

Pattern: catch up with + person / catch up over + activity.

  • I caught up with an old friend.
  • We caught up over coffee.
phrasal-prepositional verbllevarse bien con

get on well with

Meaning: have a friendly, comfortable relationship.

Use it when: describing how two people relate over time.

Pattern: get on well with + person.

  • I get on well with my neighbors.
  • Do you get on well with your coworkers?
idiomatic phrasal verbcongeniar de inmediato

hit it off (with someone)

Meaning: like and understand someone quickly after meeting.

Use it when: the first connection feels easy and positive.

Not the same as: a long friendship; it describes the initial connection.

  • We hit it off at the community dinner.
  • She hit it off with her new classmate.
separable phrasal verbinvitar a salir

ask someone out

Meaning: invite someone to go on a romantic date.

Use it when: the invitation has a romantic intention.

Word order: put the person between ask and out.

  • Jordan asked Alex out.
  • Jordan asked them out for coffee.
phrasal verbreunirse / encontrarse

meet up (with someone)

Meaning: meet at an agreed time and place.

Use it when: the meeting can be friendly, professional, or romantic.

Nuance: Unlike ask out, meet up does not automatically mean a date.

  • We are meeting up after class.
  • I met up with Lina at the café.

06 Fixed relationship expressionsUseful relationship phrases. Do not mislabel these as phrasal verbs. +
Classification: fixed / multiword expressions

These are important phrases, but they are not all phrasal verbs

These combinations are central to the unit because students need them to describe relationships. However, their grammar is different from phrasal verbs. Teach them as complete chunks with an approximate Spanish meaning, then show the pattern that students must preserve.

Pedagogical correction: Have a lot in common is not a phrasal verb. Keep in touch, get back in touch, and lose touch are better treated here as fixed relationship expressions because the teaching priority is the complete phrase and its social meaning.
fixed expressionllegar a conocer a alguien

get to know someone

Meaning: gradually learn about a person's character, interests, and life.

Approximate Spanish: llegar a conocer a alguien.

Use it when: a relationship develops over time.

Form: get to know + person; not get know.

  • I got to know Leila during the course.
  • They are getting to know each other.
fixed expressiontener mucho en común

have a lot in common

Meaning: share interests, values, experiences, or qualities.

Approximate Spanish: tener mucho en común.

Use it when: explaining why people connect.

Opposite idea: have very little / nothing in common.

  • We have a lot in common, especially music.
  • They have little in common but respect each other.
fixed expressionmantenerse en contacto

keep / stay in touch

Meaning: continue communicating after a meeting, course, job, or move.

Approximate Spanish: mantenerse en contacto.

Pattern: keep in touch with + person; stay in touch through + channel.

Variant: keep / stay in contact.

  • We keep in touch through voice messages.
  • Stay in touch with your old classmates.
fixed expressionvolver a ponerse en contacto

get back in touch

Meaning: contact someone again after communication stopped.

Approximate Spanish: volver a ponerse en contacto.

Pattern: get back in touch with + person.

Variant: get back in contact with.

  • I got back in touch with an old friend.
  • She wants to get back in contact with her former coworker.
fixed expressionperder el contacto

lose touch

Meaning: stop communicating, usually gradually and without a specific conflict.

Approximate Spanish: perder el contacto.

Pattern: lose touch with + person.

Do not assume: Losing touch does not necessarily mean the friendship ended badly.

  • We lost touch after graduation.
  • She lost contact with her former neighbors.

07 Relationship idiomsFigurative expressions. They are not phrasal verbs. +
Classification: idioms

The whole expression has a figurative meaning

Idioms are different from phrasal verbs because the complete phrase carries a figurative meaning. Do not translate them word by word. First understand the situation, then learn one natural example.

idiomestar de acuerdo / pensar igual

see eye to eye

Meaning: agree or share the same opinion.

Approximate Spanish: estar de acuerdo; pensar igual.

Use it when: discussing agreement, especially after a difference of opinion.

Pattern: see eye to eye with someone on something.

  • We do not always see eye to eye on politics.
  • I see eye to eye with my roommate on house rules.
idiomen las buenas y en las malas

through thick and thin

Meaning: during both good and difficult times.

Approximate Spanish: en las buenas y en las malas.

Use it when: emphasizing loyalty over a long period.

Register: warm, personal, and common in speech and writing.

  • My closest friend has supported me through thick and thin.
  • They stayed together through thick and thin.
idiomalguien que te apoya emocionalmente

a shoulder to cry on

Meaning: a person who listens and gives comfort when you are upset.

Approximate Spanish: alguien que te escucha y te apoya emocionalmente.

Use it when: describing emotional support, not a literal shoulder.

Pattern: be / need / offer a shoulder to cry on.

  • Nadia is always a shoulder to cry on.
  • He needed a shoulder to cry on after the conflict.
idiomamigo/a solo en los buenos momentos

a fair-weather friend

Meaning: someone who is friendly when life is easy but disappears during problems.

Approximate Spanish: amigo/a solo en los buenos momentos.

Use it when: criticizing unreliable friendship.

Nuance: This label is strongly negative, so use it carefully.

  • A fair-weather friend leaves when you need support.
  • She proved she was not a fair-weather friend.

08 Sound practiceHear who, that, linking, reduced to, and the pauses created by commas. +
Pronunciation support

Grammar becomes clearer when the listener can hear the boundaries

The goal is not to imitate a voice perfectly. Use the sound cues to make the person, the clause, and the expression easy to understand.

01

who /huː/

Start with a soft /h/ and hold the long vowel. Do not pronounce a final consonant.

the friend who called
02

that /ðæt/

Place the tongue gently between the teeth and use your voice for /ð/. This is not the /d/ sound.

the friend that called
03

Link consonant + vowel

Connect the final consonant to the next vowel so the expression flows as one unit.

get_on well · keep_in touch
04

Reduce to /tə/

In natural speech, unstressed to is often shorter and weaker.

get tə know each other
05

Pause around extra information

A non-defining clause has a light pause where the commas appear in writing.

Iris, / who was in my evening class, / is an old friend.
06

Stress the useful information

Stress the person and the key description; do not stress every word equally.

09 Complete integrated modelSee how grammar, vocabulary, expressions, and idioms work in one connected description. +
Everything together

A natural description of relationships and how two people met

This model is teaching input. The notes below show why each language choice works.

Complete modelListen before or after reading the visible model.

One of the most important people in my social circle is Camila, who lives two doors down. She is a trustworthy neighbor who helped me when I first moved into the building. At first, she seemed kind of reserved, but once we got to know each other, we discovered that we had a lot in common. We hit it off, and now we get on very well.

Camila later introduced me to Rafael, the coworker that organized our neighborhood fundraiser. He was a little bit quiet at first, but he eventually asked her out for coffee. They are seeing each other now. Camila is also a shoulder to cry on, and I know she will support me through thick and thin. Even when we are busy, we keep in touch.

relative clausevocabularytactful descriptionphrasal verbfixed expressionidiom

Why who?

Camila, who lives two doors down, has extra information after a name, so it needs commas and who. A neighbor who helped me identifies the kind of neighbor; who remains the safest people choice.

Why is that possible?

The coworker that organized our fundraiser is a defining clause with no commas. It identifies which coworker. Who would also be correct; the action organized does not force that.

Why the softeners?

Kind of reserved and a little bit quiet at first avoid absolute labels. The time phrase at first shows that the first impression may change.

Why separate labels?

Hit it off and ask out are phrasal verbs. Have a lot in common and keep in touch are fixed expressions. A shoulder to cry on is an idiom.
