Understanding the news

Identify the event, the source and what the report actually tells you.

Check the source before sharing

The editor compares the bridge in two photographs. A matching location is a useful clue; the date and original source still matter.

Audio text

The editor is comparing two photographs of the same bridge. Before sharing a report, she checks who published it, when it was taken, and what the evidence shows. A photograph can provide useful information, but it may need context. An opinion expresses a judgement. A rumour is information that has not been confirmed.

The title of a news story. It introduces the main point.

The person, document or organization that provides information.

A person who gathers information and presents news.

Someone who saw or experienced an event.

Something a person or organization says formally.

Information that supports or challenges a claim.

02Facts, opinions and rumoursKeep confirmed information separate from interpretation and unverified claims.+
Meaning, form and use

Facts, opinions and rumours

Keep confirmed information separate from interpretation and unverified claims.

Something that is true and can be checked.

A personal judgement or belief.

Information being shared without confirmation.

An assertion that needs to be checked; it may be true or false.

MessageWhat kind?How to handle it
The library is closed. The council posted a closure notice.A checkable report with a named source.Check that the notice is current and refers to this library.
I think the repairs should be the council’s priority.An opinion.Explain the reason; do not present the preference as a fact.
Someone online says the library will never reopen.An unverified claim or rumour.Find the original source and supporting evidence before repeating it.

Reporting a statement does not confirm its content

“A resident said…” tells us who made the statement. It does not guarantee that the statement is accurate. A claim may later be confirmed or disproved.

Connection with Unit 5

Satire uses humour or exaggeration to comment on an issue. A surprising headline is not automatically satire, and satire is not the same thing as an unverified rumour.

03What did they say?Compare the speaker’s original words with a later report.+
Meaning, form and use

What did they say?

Compare the speaker’s original words with a later report.

Maya speaks to the reporter

Direct speech: quotation marks show Maya’s exact words. I refers to Maya.

Audio text

This is a fictional teaching example. A journalist interviews Maya outside the library after a storm. Maya says, "I am worried about the library." The journalist writes down her exact words. This is direct speech. The speaker uses I because she is talking about herself. The quotation preserves the words she actually said.

The reporter tells his editor

Reported speech: the reporter gives Maya’s message from his point of view. He refers to her as she.

Audio text

Later, the journalist tells his editor about the interview. Maya said that she was worried about the library. This is reported speech. I changes to she because the journalist is talking about Maya. Am changes to was because he is reporting her earlier words from a later point in time. The meaning should remain accurate.

Original wordsLater reportReason
IsheThe journalist is talking about Maya.
amwasHe looks back at her earlier statement.
“I am worried…”She said that she was worried…The whole sentence is a report, not a direct quotation.
Keep the message faithful

Do not turn “I am worried” into “She was terrified.” Changing the grammar should not exaggerate the person’s meaning.

04Say, tell and reported statementsBuild a report with a reporting verb and a complete idea.+
Meaning, form and use

Say, tell and reported statements

Build a report with a reporting verb and a complete idea.

PatternModelWhat to notice
Subject + said + (that) + ideaMaya said (that) she was worried.Said does not need a listener here.
Subject + told + listener + (that) + ideaMaya told the reporter (that) she was worried.Name the listener after told: me, us, the reporter.
Subject + said + to + listenerMaya said to the reporter, “I am worried.”Use to when you name a listener after said.

That is optional

Maya said that she was worried.
Maya said she was worried.

Both are correct. That connects the reporting verb to the message; it does not replace the subject.

Negatives belong to the message

“I am not ready.” → She said she was not ready.

Keep the negative meaning. “She didn’t say she was ready” describes what she did not say; it is a different message.

AvoidUseWhy
She said me the news.She told me the news.Tell takes a listener directly.
She told that she was worried.She told us that she was worried.For a reported statement, told needs a listener.
She said was worried.She said she was worried.The reported idea needs its own subject.
05What changes when we report?Adjust the grammar to the speaker, the time and the place of the report.+
Meaning, form and use

What changes when we report?

Adjust the grammar to the speaker, the time and the place of the report.

Backshift: looking back from a later time

With a past reporting verb such as said, we often move the original verb one step back. Start with these common changes. A past form in a report does not prove that the situation has ended.

Direct speechLater reportChange
am → was
will → would
can → could
“We need help.”They said they needed help.present simple → past simple
“We are checking the roof.”They said they were checking the roof.present continuous → past continuous
ReferenceExampleCheck the situation
Person“I lost my bag.” → Maya said she had lost her bag.I and my refer to Maya, so the reporter uses she and her.
TimeOn Tuesday: “tomorrow” → reporting on Thursday: “the following day”The original tomorrow meant Wednesday. Do not accidentally change the date.
Place“here” → “there” when reporting from another placeIf you are still in the same place, here may remain appropriate.

Still true now

If the library remains closed, a present form can show that the information is still current. Was still closed is also possible when reporting the earlier statement.

Reporting immediately

The singer says she can perform on Saturday.

With says in the present, we normally do not backshift just because we are reporting. Keep time words when their reference has not changed.

Extra support · earlier past events

Present perfect often becomes past perfect: “I have checked the source.” → She said she had checked the source.

Past simple can become past perfect: “I saw the tree fall.” → She said she had seen the tree fall. Past simple is also possible when the timing is already clear.

Past perfect stays past perfect. Would, could, should and might usually stay the same. These are extensions; master the main patterns first.

06Discuss political and entertainment newsAttribute the information, explain your view and respond respectfully.+
Meaning, form and use

Discuss political and entertainment news

Attribute the information, explain your view and respond respectfully.

A council promise

Report who made the promise and when. Later, check what actually happened.

Audio text

In this fictional news story, a council spokesperson discusses repairs to a community library. She says, "We will publish the repair plan tomorrow." Two days later, a journalist reports that the spokesperson said they would publish the repair plan the following day. The journalist should then check whether the plan was actually published. Reporting a promise does not prove that it was fulfilled.

A concert announcement

A statement from the singer is stronger evidence than an anonymous post about her plans.

Audio text

In this fictional interview, a singer announces a community concert. She says, "I can perform on Saturday." Later, the interviewer reports that the singer said she could perform on Saturday. An announcement from the singer is different from an unconfirmed rumour. When discussing entertainment news, explain where the information came from and separate the person's words from your own opinion.

A group responsible for decisions in a town or local area.

Someone who speaks on behalf of a group or organization.

A public statement giving news or information.

PurposeUseful language
Name the sourceAccording to the council… / The spokesperson said…
Explain controversySome residents support the plan because… Others disagree because…
Give a reasonI think the library should reopen soon because students use it to study.
Disagree respectfullyI understand your point, but I would like to see the repair report first.
Ask for evidenceWhere did that information come from? Has it been confirmed?
A resolved model

“The council announced a repair plan. Some residents think it is too slow because the library is their main study space. Others support the inspection because they want the building checked carefully. I would compare the proposed dates and the inspection findings before deciding.”

Discuss the issue, not private lives

In these fictional examples, focus on public statements, decisions and evidence. You can explain different viewpoints without sharing your own political affiliation or spreading personal rumours.

07Natural disasters and their effectsName the event and describe the effects without inventing details.+
Meaning, form and use

Natural disasters and their effects

Name the event and describe the effects without inventing details.

A sudden shaking of the ground.

The earthquake damaged several buildings.

Audio text

An earthquake is a sudden shaking of the ground. In this fictional scene, an earthquake damaged several buildings. An assessment team inspected the area from behind a barrier. Notice the past forms: damaged and inspected. The picture shows damage to buildings, but it does not tell us how many people were affected. Do not invent information that the source does not provide.

Water covering land that is normally dry.

The river overflowed and flooded the street.

Audio text

A flood happens when water covers land that is normally dry. In this fictional scene, the river overflowed and flooded a residential street. A rescue crew travelled through the street in a boat. The word flooded describes what happened. The phrase a rescue crew names the people responding. We need a reliable source before reporting numbers or causes.

A powerful tropical storm with strong winds and heavy rain.

The hurricane damaged homes near the coast.

Audio text

A hurricane is a powerful tropical storm with strong winds and heavy rain. In this fictional scene, the wind bent the palm trees and rain reduced visibility. A reporter could describe the visible conditions, but the image alone cannot tell us the wind speed. Compare the verb forms: bend, bent; reduce, reduced.

A long period with much less rain than a place needs.

Water levels fell during the drought.

Audio text

A drought is a long period with much less rain than a place needs. In this fictional scene, the reservoir has very little water and the ground is dry and cracked. To report a completed event, we could say that water levels fell during the dry season. Fell is the past form of fall.

An uncontrolled fire spreading through vegetation.

The wildfire burned part of the wooded hillside.

Audio text

A wildfire is an uncontrolled fire that spreads through vegetation. In this fictional scene, a fire burned part of a wooded hillside. Firefighters assessed the situation from a distant observation point. Burned and assessed describe completed actions. Smoke and flames are visible, but the image does not establish how the fire started.

Rock, soil or other material moving down a slope.

The landslide blocked the mountain road.

Audio text

A landslide happens when rock, soil, or other material moves down a slope. In this fictional scene, rocks and soil blocked a mountain road. Workers closed the road and inspected the damage. First, the material slid down the hillside. Then, the road was closed. These expressions help the listener follow the order of events.

Harm to buildings, objects or places. Usually uncountable in this meaning.

A team that helps people in danger.

A place that provides protection or temporary accommodation.

A message about possible danger or a problem.

Things people need, such as water, food or equipment.

A period when the electricity supply stops.

A tree that has fallen to the ground.

Damage or damages?

For physical harm, use damage: “The storm caused damage.” To count, say “three damaged buildings.” Damages has a separate legal meaning; it is not the plural we need here.

An image cannot confirm every detail

These are generated teaching scenes. A real report needs verified dates, locations, causes and figures. Describe what is visible and identify what still needs confirmation.

Reporting what happened

Use past simple for completed events and connectors for their order.

Base formPast formNews example
hithitA storm hit the town on Monday.
fallfellA tree fell near the library.
damagedamagedThe storm damaged the roof.
rescuerescuedThe crew rescued two residents.

Affirmative

Subject + past verb
The storm damaged the roof.

Use a finished-time expression when known: on Monday, yesterday, last night.

Negative and question

The storm did not damage the school.
Did the storm damage the school?

After did or didn’t, use the base form. Avoid “did damaged.”

SequenceModel
FirstFirst, the storm hit Greenford.
ThenThen, a tree fell near the entrance.
Later / after thatLater, workers inspected the roof.
FinallyFinally, the council issued an update.
Two different jobs

The storm damaged the roof directly reports a past event. Maya said the storm had damaged the roof reports Maya’s earlier statement. The second sentence also identifies the source.

Hear the endings

Reported ends in /ɪd/; damaged and rescued end in /d/. Keep the ending audible without adding an extra syllable to every past verb.

Instructions and action plans

Distinguish direct instructions, advice and a report of someone’s instructions.

An information plan

Identify who provides the updates, what the group should check and how confirmed information will be shared.

Audio text

In this fictional classroom scenario, a coordinator explains a community information plan. First, identify the official source of updates. Then, check the time and location of each message. Finally, share the confirmed information clearly. The coordinator told the group to check official updates. After told, name the listener, then use to and the base form of the verb.

FunctionFormExample
InstructionBase verbCheck official alerts.
Negative instructionDo not + base verb
Adviceshould + base verbYou should check the source before sharing the update.
Reported instructiontold + listener + to + base verb
Reported negative instructiontold + listener + not to + base verb
To check, not to checked

After told someone to, use the base verb: “She told us to check.” The past tense is in told. Use not before to for the negative: “She told us not to enter.”

10Expressions and a complete news bulletinBring together a clear source, ordered events and faithful reported speech.+
Meaning, form and use

Expressions and a complete news bulletin

Bring together a clear source, ordered events and faithful reported speech.

What is a phrasal verb?

A verb and a particle work together as one expression: carry + out means perform. In the separable examples below, a pronoun goes between the verb and particle: carry it out.

What is an idiom?

An expression has a conventional meaning that may go beyond its individual words. Take it with a grain of salt asks the listener to be cautious; it is not about food.

Phrasal verb

Learn or discover information.

averiguar

Grammar: Transitive and separable in this noun-object pattern.

Model: find out the cause / find the cause out / find it out

Also: find out what happened. Keep a full what-clause after out.

Sound: Link find‿out; give out a clear stress.

Phrasal verb

Examine something to see whether it is accurate or useful.

comprobar o investigar

Grammar: Transitive and separable in this meaning.

Model: check out the claim / check the claim out / check it out

Conversational. Verify is a more formal alternative.

Sound: Link check‿out; keep the final /t/.

Phrasal verb

Perform or complete a task.

llevar a cabo

Grammar: Transitive and separable.

Model: carry out an inspection / carry an inspection out / carry it out

Common in formal reports. The first noun order is often smoother with long objects.

Sound: Link carry‿out; stress the key task.

Phrasal verb

Draw attention to something.

señalar o destacar

Grammar: Transitive and separable.

Model: point out the damage / point the damage out / point it out

It can also introduce a clause: she pointed out that the road was closed.

Sound: Keep point and out connected without losing the /t/.

Phrasal verb

Remove confusion or make something understandable.

aclarar

Grammar: Transitive and separable in this meaning.

Model: clear up the confusion / clear the confusion up / clear it up

Here it means clarify, not improve weather or tidy a room.

Sound: Link clear‿up; stress up.

Idiom

Information passed from one person to another.

de boca en boca

Grammar: Fixed idiomatic expression.

Model: The rumour spread by word of mouth.

Everyday language. Being widely repeated does not confirm a report.

Sound: Say by word of mouth as one group.

Idiom

Attract prominent attention in the news.

ser noticia o acaparar titulares

Grammar: Idiomatic expression.

Model: The concert announcement made headlines.

Common in media reporting; it does not mean physically creating a headline.

Sound: Stress headlines; make changes to made in the past.

Idiom

Treat information with some doubt.

tomarlo con reservas

Grammar: Idiom.

Model: The story has no named source, so take it with a grain of salt.

Informal. It expresses caution, not proof that a claim is false.

Sound: Keep with a grain of salt together as one group.

Common error

Use “check it out,” not “check out it.” For a noun, both “check out the claim” and “check the claim out” work. The pronoun rule applies to the separable meanings shown here.

A short news report or official update.

Model bulletin · a fictional report

Audio text

This is a fictional news bulletin for language practice. Good evening. A storm hit Greenford on Monday and damaged the community library. A tree fell near the entrance, and workers closed the building. Maya, a local resident, told our reporter that she was worried about the library. At a briefing on Tuesday, the council spokesperson said they would publish a repair plan the following day. Workers then inspected the roof. The cause of the roof damage is still under investigation. We will update this report when more information is confirmed.
