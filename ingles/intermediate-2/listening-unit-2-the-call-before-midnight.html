Language noticing

Hear how the unit language carries the decision

“I've dreamed of doing this for years, and I hope I can use what I learn...”

Dream or hope?

Dream of + -ing names Salomé's long-term aspiration. Hope introduces a future result she considers genuinely possible.

“If I accepted the scholarship, I would have to leave...”

Why the second conditional?

The decision is still imaginary. The past form accepted marks the condition; would have to presents its possible consequence.

“I regret refusing it... I wish I had asked...”

Two ways to express regret

Regret + -ing names the action she feels sorry about. Wish + past perfect imagines a different past action.

“If I were you, I'd think this offer over.”

Advice plus a phrasal verb

If I were you gives diplomatic advice. Think something over means consider it carefully before deciding.

“They might work out a later starting date.”

How certain is the compromise?

Might keeps the possibility uncertain. Work out means find or arrange a successful solution.

“You're at a crossroads... weigh the consequences.”

Idiom or fixed expression?

At a crossroads is an idiom for an important choice. Weigh the consequences is a fixed expression for comparing likely results.

Salomé: Mr. Vega, sorry to call this late, but I have to answer before midnight. A foundation offered me a full scholarship to study urban design in another city. I've dreamed of doing this for years, and I hope I can use what I learn to improve public spaces.

Mr. Vega: That sounds important. What is making the decision difficult?

Salomé: I promised to lead our neighborhood library project next month. If I accepted the scholarship, I would have to leave before the project begins. The team is counting on me.

Salomé: I feel torn. This could be a dream come true, but I don't want the neighborhood team to feel abandoned.

Mr. Vega: So you're at a crossroads. Which part worries you most?

Mr. Vega: Your concern shows responsibility, but it doesn't mean you must reject the opportunity.

Salomé: Making the same mistake twice. Two years ago, I turned down an internship. I regret refusing it so quickly, and I wish I had asked whether the dates were flexible.

Mr. Vega: If I were you, I'd think this offer over before saying no. You could call the foundation and explain your commitment. They might work out a later starting date.

Salomé: And if they say no?

Mr. Vega: Then weigh the consequences. Which decision could you live with? A responsible choice begins with all the facts.

Salomé: Wait. It's eleven fifteen, and they just sent another email.

Mr. Vega: Open it.

Salomé: The first sentence... it changes everything.

Speak from what you heard

Give Salomé one careful recommendation

Speak for 45 seconds. State what you would do, give one reason and acknowledge one consequence. Do not invent the email's content.

Example interaction

Teacher: What would you do if you were Salomé?

Student: If I were Salomé, I would call the foundation before deciding. She could explain the library project and ask about a later start.

Teacher: What consequence should she consider?

Student: If the dates cannot change, she might disappoint the team. However, turning the scholarship down too quickly could create another regret.
